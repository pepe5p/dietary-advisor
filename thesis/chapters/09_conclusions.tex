\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{cha:conclusions}

This closing chapter states what the work established, what it could not establish, and what should be attempted next. The short version is that the cheapest intervention studied here was also the most effective one, the most elaborate was the least cost-effective, and the largest single accuracy difference in the entire evaluation came from a configuration setting rather than from either mechanism the thesis set out to study. That last point is worth carrying away independently of the hypotheses: a weak model allowed to reason harder outperformed several stronger ones, and the effort setting is the parameter a practitioner is least likely to tune deliberately.

\section{Summary of findings}
\label{sec:conclusions-summary}

This thesis set out to measure how much two grounding and review mechanisms -- a deterministic macronutrient totaller and a critique-and-refine loop -- contribute to the reliability of an LLM-based one-day meal planner, on top of a food knowledge base the planner is already required to query rather than guess from. The resulting system enforces that requirement structurally: its language model never has a field in which to write a nutrient value, so every number in a delivered plan is traceable to a real food database entry and an exact arithmetic computation (\Cref{sec:impl-portion-ref,sec:design-no-invented-foods}).

Measured against that grounded baseline, enabling the deterministic totaller alone nearly halved macronutrient error, from \qty{27.41}{\percent} to \qty{14.13}{\percent}, for about a third more runtime -- H1 supported, and by the cheapest mechanism tested. Enabling the critique-and-refine loop improved accuracy further, to \qty{12.43}{\percent} in combination with the totaller, but roughly tripled wall-clock time and produced no measurable improvement in either judge's preference or safety scores, so H2 is supported on accuracy and unsupported on adherence. H3 is supported once capability is understood to include reasoning effort as well as model choice, though not in the form originally anticipated: the weakest model did not benefit most from grounding and critique, but it benefited enormously from being allowed to think longer, its error falling by roughly two thirds and bringing it close to models several times slower, while the same setting left stronger models flat or slightly worse.

Three secondary findings are worth carrying forward. Residual macronutrient error concentrates disproportionately in fibre, tracing back to that nutrient's uneven coverage in the underlying food data rather than to any weakness in the aggregation, which places a floor on what arithmetic grounding can achieve. The qualitative metrics barely separate the ablation variants, and the run-to-run variability measured in \Cref{sec:results-stability} is of the same order as the differences between them, so those particular comparisons cannot bear weight. And the two judges, while agreeing on almost every ranking, disagree systematically on absolute scores and -- more troublingly -- on where allergen and diet-pattern compliance actually slipped, which suggests hard exclusions deserve a deterministic checker rather than a judged score.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusions-limitations}

The evaluation is bounded in scope in ways that should temper how broadly its conclusions are read. The scenario set is five profiles, chosen for the clarity of their associated dietary guidance and for the variety of demands they place on the planner rather than for coverage of the conditions a real deployment would encounter. Both judges are language models: pairing two vendors makes family-specific bias visible, and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows it did not disturb the rankings, but a bias the two share would still be invisible, and no human expert reviewed a single plan. Reasoning-effort levels are vendor-defined labels rather than calibrated quantities, so effort comparisons hold within a model but only indicate a direction across models. Finally, the system produces a single, non-interactive plan per session; nothing here speaks to how these mechanisms would behave across a multi-turn, incrementally edited planning conversation.

\section{Further work}
\label{sec:conclusions-future-work}

Several directions follow directly from the limitations above and from the paths not taken in \Cref{sec:impl-dead-ends}.

\textbf{A deterministic checker for hard exclusions.} The most immediate improvement suggested by the results is to stop asking a judge whether an allergen was avoided. The two judges disagree about exactly this (\Cref{sec:results-error-analysis}), and allergen and diet-pattern compliance is checkable mechanically against the same database entries the plan already references, in the same spirit as the no-invented-foods contract. Moving it out of the judged score would both harden the system and free the qualitative rubric to concentrate on things that genuinely require judgement.

\textbf{Human expert evaluation.} The qualitative scoring relies entirely on an LLM-as-a-judge protocol; validating a sample of its scores against a registered dietitian's independent judgement would establish how far the judges' shared biases (\Cref{sec:background-evaluation}) affect the specific rubric and scenarios used here, which no purely automated evaluation can determine on its own.

\textbf{Calibrating reasoning effort across vendors.} The effort finding in \Cref{sec:results-effort} is the most actionable result of this work and also the least precisely measured, because each provider names its own levels. Establishing a comparable measure -- reasoning tokens consumed, say, rather than a vendor's label -- would turn a directional observation into an engineering guideline for choosing a model and effort setting under a latency budget.

\textbf{Multi-turn interactive refinement.} Extending the system to accept a follow-up request against an already-generated plan, rather than only a fresh session each time, was set aside in \Cref{sec:impl-dead-ends} as orthogonal to this thesis's research question; it remains a natural and comparatively well-scoped extension, since the critique-and-refine loop's revise-and-recheck pattern (\Cref{sec:design-reflection}) already provides most of the machinery a human-in-the-loop version of the same pattern would need.

\textbf{Model-generated food descriptions for retrieval.} \Cref{sec:impl-dead-ends} also set aside having a language model author a richer description of each food catalogue entry specifically to improve the text embedded for dense retrieval (\Cref{sec:impl-hybrid-search}), instead of the fixed field-concatenation rule used throughout this work. Measuring whether such descriptions actually improve retrieval quality, and by how much relative to the added offline build cost, is left as an open question.

\textbf{Elicitation of missing profile data.} The system currently assumes a complete, already-known user profile; a natural extension is a short elicitation dialogue that asks a small number of targeted follow-up questions when a profile is incomplete in a way that would materially affect the computed nutrient targets, rather than requiring every field to be supplied up front.

\textbf{Robustness to prompt injection.} Because the system accepts a free-form session request as part of its input, a request crafted to redirect the underlying model away from meal planning entirely -- towards an unrelated task embedded in the request text -- was not evaluated in this thesis and is a natural robustness question for a system intended for real-world use, distinct from the nutritional-accuracy focus of the evaluation reported here.

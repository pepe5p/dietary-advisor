\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{cha:conclusions}

This closing chapter states what the work established, what it could not establish, and what should be attempted next. Three points summarise it. Of the two mechanisms studied, the simpler one -- a deterministic calculator the model may consult -- accounted for the entire accuracy gain, for the smaller increase in run time, while the more elaborate critique-and-refine loop added nothing measurable on top of it for roughly triple the run time. The largest single accuracy difference measured anywhere in the evaluation came from neither mechanism but from a configuration setting: a reasoning-effort level raised on the weakest model cut its macronutrient error by roughly three quarters. And the two things this system is asked to get right -- the numbers and the request -- proved to rank its configurations differently, so a decision made on either one alone is a decision made on half the evidence.

\section{Summary of findings}
\label{sec:conclusions-summary}

This thesis set out to measure how much two grounding and review mechanisms -- a deterministic macronutrient totaller and a critique-and-refine loop -- contribute to the reliability of an LLM-based one-day meal planner, on top of a food knowledge base the planner is already required to query rather than guess from. The resulting system enforces that requirement structurally: its language model never has a field in which to write a nutrient value, so every number in a delivered plan is traceable to a real food database entry and an exact arithmetic computation (\Cref{sec:impl-portion-ref,sec:design-no-invented-foods}).

Measured against that grounded baseline, enabling the deterministic totaller cut macronutrient error from \qty{13.46}{\percent} to \qty{3.02}{\percent} for about a third more runtime, improved every one of the five scenarios and reduced the run-to-run standard deviation by an order of magnitude, which supports H1~through the cheaper of the two mechanisms. The critique-and-refine loop added nothing measurable on top of it: \qty{3.25}{\percent} against \qty{3.02}{\percent} is within the standard error of both means, no judge's preference or safety score responded to it, and it roughly tripled wall-clock time, so H2~is unsupported on accuracy as well as on adherence once the totaller is present. H3~is supported in all three of its parts -- plan quality varied with the model, from \qty{1.87}{\percent} to \qty{13.39}{\percent} mean error and \num{0.808} to \num{0.730} soft aggregate across the four compared; it varied with the reasoning-effort level, monotonically from \qty{8.66}{\percent} to \qty{2.64}{\percent} pooled across models and within each one individually; and raising the weakest model's effort recovered roughly three quarters of its accuracy deficit. The practical caveat is that this was still not the right trade: the strongest model at its \emph{lowest} effort beat the effort-boosted weak model on both dimensions for \qty{14}{\second} more per plan.

Three secondary findings are worth carrying forward. The two evaluation dimensions do not order the configurations the same way, so neither is a proxy for the other: the two configurations tied for lowest macronutrient error differ sharply in how well the judges think their plans answer the request, and reasoning effort improves accuracy monotonically while leaving preference satisfaction flat past its middle setting. Fibre is the one nutrient this system cannot currently promise, not because the arithmetic fails but because the source data is too often silent about it, which is why it is recorded per plan and kept out of the error aggregate. And the two judges, while agreeing on almost every ranking, disagree systematically on absolute scores and, less excusably for a question with an objectively checkable answer, on where allergen and diet-pattern compliance actually slipped, which suggests hard exclusions deserve a deterministic checker rather than a judged score.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusions-limitations}

The evaluation is bounded in scope in ways that should temper how broadly its conclusions are read. The scenario set is five profiles, chosen for the clarity of their associated dietary guidance and for the variety of demands they place on the planner rather than for coverage of the conditions a real deployment would encounter. Both judges are language models: pairing two vendors makes family-specific bias visible, and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows it did not disturb the rankings, but a bias the two share would still be invisible, and no human expert reviewed a single plan. Reasoning-effort levels are vendor-defined labels rather than calibrated quantities, so effort comparisons hold within a model but only indicate a direction across models. The reported error figures cover four nutrients rather than five, fibre being excluded for the data-coverage reason given in \Cref{sec:methodology-quantitative}, so they certify nothing about how closely a plan meets a fibre target. Finally, the system produces a single, non-interactive plan per session; nothing here speaks to how these mechanisms would behave across a multi-turn, incrementally edited planning conversation.

\section{Further work}
\label{sec:conclusions-future-work}

Several directions follow directly from the limitations above and from the paths not taken in \Cref{sec:impl-dead-ends}.

\textbf{A deterministic checker for hard exclusions.} The most immediate improvement suggested by the results is to stop asking a judge whether an allergen was avoided. The two judges disagree about exactly this (\Cref{sec:results-error-analysis}), and allergen and diet-pattern compliance is checkable mechanically against the same database entries the plan already references, in the same spirit as the no-invented-foods contract. Moving it out of the judged score would both harden the system and free the qualitative rubric to concentrate on things that genuinely require judgement.

\textbf{Human expert evaluation.} The qualitative scoring relies entirely on an LLM-as-a-judge protocol; validating a sample of its scores against a registered dietitian's independent judgement would establish how far the judges' shared biases (\Cref{sec:background-evaluation}) affect the specific rubric and scenarios used here, which no purely automated evaluation can determine on its own.

\textbf{Calibrating reasoning effort across vendors.} The effort finding in \Cref{sec:results-effort} has direct practical consequences and is also the least precisely measured result reported here, because each provider names its own levels. Establishing a comparable measure -- reasoning tokens consumed, say, rather than a vendor's label -- would turn a directional observation into an engineering guideline for choosing a model and effort setting under a latency budget.

\textbf{Multi-turn interactive refinement.} Extending the system to accept a follow-up request against an already-generated plan, rather than only a fresh session each time, was set aside in \Cref{sec:impl-dead-ends} as orthogonal to this thesis's research question; it remains a natural and comparatively well-scoped extension, since the critique-and-refine loop's revise-and-recheck pattern (\Cref{sec:design-reflection}) already provides most of the machinery a human-in-the-loop version of the same pattern would need.

\textbf{Model-generated food descriptions for retrieval.} \Cref{sec:impl-dead-ends} also set aside having a language model author a richer description of each food catalogue entry specifically to improve the text embedded for dense retrieval (\Cref{sec:impl-hybrid-search}), instead of the fixed field-concatenation rule used throughout this work. Measuring whether such descriptions actually improve retrieval quality, and by how much relative to the added offline build cost, is left as an open question.

\textbf{Elicitation of missing profile data.} The system currently assumes a complete, already-known user profile; a natural extension is a short elicitation dialogue that asks a small number of targeted follow-up questions when a profile is incomplete in a way that would materially affect the computed nutrient targets, rather than requiring every field to be supplied up front.

\textbf{Robustness to prompt injection.} Because the system accepts a free-form session request as part of its input, a request crafted to redirect the underlying model away from meal planning entirely -- towards an unrelated task embedded in the request text -- was not evaluated in this thesis and is a natural robustness question for a system intended for real-world use, distinct from the nutritional-accuracy focus of the evaluation reported here.

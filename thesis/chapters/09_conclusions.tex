\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{cha:conclusions}

This thesis measured how much two mechanisms -- a deterministic nutrient totaller and a critique-and-refine loop -- contribute to the reliability of an LLM-based one-day meal planner built on a queried food knowledge base. The system enforces grounding structurally: the language model has no field in which to write a nutrient value, so every number in a delivered plan traces to a real food-database entry and an exact computation (\Cref{sec:impl-portion-ref,sec:design-no-invented-foods}).

H1~(RQ1) is supported: the cheaper of the two mechanisms, the totaller, cuts energy and macronutrient error sharply in every scenario and makes plans more predictable. H2~(RQ2) is not supported as an increment on top of the totaller: no further effect of the critique-and-refine loop is detectable at this sample size on either evaluation dimension. On its own the loop does cut macro error, roughly in half against the unaided baseline, but it neither separates from that baseline on the dimension H2~was stated in -- adherence to constraints and preferences -- nor escapes costing roughly three times the wall-clock time. H3~(RQ3) is supported in all three parts on accuracy, and in its first two on preference -- plan quality varies with model and reasoning effort, and raising a weaker model's effort recovers a substantial part of the gap, though preference satisfaction stops improving past the middle effort setting -- with the caveat that spending the same latency on a stronger model at low effort still dominates (\Cref{sec:results-ablation,sec:results-model-comparison,sec:results-overhead}).

Those three verdicts sit on a two-dimensional evaluation that would have been misleading if collapsed. The configurations that tie for lowest macro error are not the ones the judges prefer, and raising reasoning effort improves accuracy monotonically while leaving preference satisfaction flat past its middle setting. A system builder who optimised only for the totaller's metric would have declared the strongest high-effort model the winner; the qualitative series and the wall-clock figures reverse that choice. On the loop they point the same way as the accuracy metric: optional, and expensive. The two judges, while agreeing on almost every ranking, disagree systematically on absolute scores and on where allergen and diet-pattern compliance slipped (\Cref{sec:results-stability}). That disagreement suggests hard exclusions deserve a deterministic checker, not a judged score.

The engineering record is part of the result as well. The no-invented-foods contract, the coverage-aware totaller, and the batched lookup tool were all forced by artefacts that looked valid in a typed field. They are why the food knowledge base could be treated as a premise of the study instead of as another ablation flag: without a resolvable code there is nothing for \ref{req:no-invented-nutrients} to enforce and nothing for \Cref{def:macro-error} to measure.

Those verdicts should be read inside the bounds of \Cref{sec:results-threats}: a five-scenario grid, LLM judges without a human expert, vendor-defined effort labels, a single non-interactive session, and a snapshot of hosted models that will not stay still.

\section{Further work}
\label{sec:conclusions-future-work}

Several directions follow directly from the limitations of \Cref{sec:results-threats}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Retrieval-augmented clinical guidance.} The dietary guardrails in every agent's prompt are stated inline because they apply near-universally to a healthy adult (\Cref{sec:design-agents}); guidance that varies with a diagnosed condition does not fit that reasoning, and the chronic-condition scenarios are where it would matter most. Retrieving condition-specific guidance from a curated clinical corpus, as \textsc{DietQA} applies to recipe data (\Cref{sec:background-related-systems}), would add a third grounding mechanism whose contribution the same ablation design could quantify.
    \item \textbf{Probability-weighted judge scores.} A judge that exposed token probabilities would yield G-Eval's weighted score directly (\Cref{sec:methodology-qualitative}), removing both the sampling cost of the repetition axis and the residual judge noise that \Cref{sec:results-stability} has to report around it.
    \item \textbf{Pairwise comparison between configurations.} Every qualitative score here is absolute: a plan is read against a rubric without reference to any other plan. Pairwise LLM judging has been shown to agree with expert human preference on \qty{85}{\percent} of non-tied comparisons of chatbot responses on MT-Bench (\Cref{sec:background-evaluation})~\cite{zheng2023judging}, and transferring that protocol to two plans for the same profile would sharpen the comparisons this thesis found hardest to call -- the configurations that tied on accuracy but not on preference -- at the cost of a comparison count quadratic in the number of variants and of randomising presentation order against the documented position bias.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric critic model.} Whether a critic benefits from being a stronger model than the generator it reviews was left open in \Cref{sec:impl-critic-design}. Since the critique-and-refine loop's null result (\Cref{sec:results-ablation}) was obtained under an equal-capability critic, it bounds only what a self-review by an equally capable model achieves.
    \item \textbf{Multi-turn interactive refinement.} Accepting a follow-up request against an already-generated plan was excluded from the single-session scope of \Cref{sec:vision-usage-scenario}; it is a well-scoped extension, since the critique-and-refine loop's revise-and-recheck pattern (\Cref{sec:design-reflection}) already provides most of the machinery a human-in-the-loop version would need.
    \item \textbf{Model-generated food descriptions for retrieval.} The text embedded for dense retrieval is assembled by a fixed field-concatenation rule (\Cref{sec:impl-hybrid-search}). Having a language model instead author a richer description of each food-catalogue entry could normalise inconsistent crowd-sourced data, at the cost of an offline LLM-dependent build step; whether it improves retrieval quality enough to justify that cost is an open question.
    \item \textbf{Elicitation of missing profile data.} The system assumes a complete user profile. A short elicitation dialogue could ask targeted follow-up questions when a profile is incomplete in a way that materially affects the computed nutrient targets.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Results and discussion}
\label{cha:results}

This chapter reports what the two experiments of \Cref{cha:methodology} measured and what those measurements license as conclusions.

The problem is two-dimensional: a plan must hit a numeric target and must satisfy what the user asked for, and \Cref{cha:methodology} measures those separately because neither is recoverable from the other. Neither dimension is reported on its own, and where the two disagree -- which happens in the model comparison and again for reasoning effort -- the disagreement is the finding, plotted directly in \Cref{fig:results-tradeoff-mean}.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:results-setup}

Both grids ran to completion: Experiment~1, the component ablation (\Cref{sec:methodology-variants}), with \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} as its fixed model, and Experiment~2, the model comparison (\Cref{sec:methodology-model-grid}), with the system fixed at the full variant. Every mean reported here rests on the full complement of repetitions.

Every plan was scored by both judges of \Cref{sec:methodology-qualitative}: \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} as Judge~1 and \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash-0731} as Judge~2. Judge~1 shares a model family with one of the twelve compared configurations, the overlap the second judge exists to expose; \Cref{sec:results-stability} reports whether it distorted anything. Judge-independent metrics -- energy and macronutrient error, iteration count, totaller calls and elapsed time -- are quoted without a judge attribution throughout.

\section{Component ablation}
\label{sec:results-ablation}

Experiment~1 compares the four variants of \Cref{sec:methodology-variants} on a fixed \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} (\Cref{tab:results-ablation-summary}, \Cref{fig:results-ablation-mae}). Enabling the totaller alone reduces macro error (\Cref{def:macro-error}) from \qty{13.46}{\percent} to \qty{3.02}{\percent}, more than fourfold, and cuts MSE from \num{496} to \num{17}. The reflection loop alone reaches \qty{5.56}{\percent}, a smaller improvement bought at roughly three times the wall-clock time.

Enabling both yields the central result: at \qty{3.25}{\percent} macro error the full variant is not measurably different from the totaller alone. The gap is \qty{0.23}{\percent}, against standard errors of \qty{0.35}{\percent} and \qty{0.73}{\percent}, and their MSE figures run the other way, \num{25} against \num{17}. Once a deterministic aggregator checks the numbers, the critique-and-refine loop has no accuracy left to contribute; the two mechanisms are not complementary on this evidence. Its cost is taken up in \Cref{sec:results-overhead}.

Run-to-run variation matters as much as the mean; where it is the point the text quotes the population standard deviation over individual runs, not the standard error the figures show. The baseline's standard deviation is \qty{14.76}{\percent} -- larger than its own mean error, and larger than the entire mean error of any other variant -- while the three remaining variants sit between \qty{1.34}{\percent} and \qty{4.32}{\percent}. Pooled this way, that figure mixes sampling noise with scenario difficulty and is not a clean measure of nondeterminism, but its point survives the separation: most of the baseline's deviation comes from a single scenario in which three repetitions of an identical request disagreed by nearly a factor of seven. An ungrounded plan is not merely worse on average but substantially less predictable -- for a system whose failures are silent, the more serious property.

On the second dimension the ablation is a null result (\Cref{fig:results-ablation-soft}). Both judges place all four variants within a narrow band -- roughly \numrange{0.79}{0.81} for Judge~1 and \numrange{0.80}{0.84} for Judge~2 -- so neither registers the fourfold accuracy difference of \Cref{fig:results-ablation-mae}. Neither mechanism changes how well a plan satisfies what the user asked for; the accuracy findings are claims about one dimension only. The two series also show a consistent offset: Judge~2 rates essentially every variant higher than Judge~1, by a margin comparable to the differences between the variants themselves -- the central caution of this chapter's qualitative results, taken up in \Cref{sec:results-stability}.

\Cref{tab:results-per-scenario} breaks macro error down by scenario, where the two mechanisms part company most clearly. The totaller improves on the baseline in all five scenarios, and most where the unaided model was worst: the lactose-intolerant-athlete scenario falls from \qty{34.1}{\percent} to \qty{3.8}{\percent}, while the diabetes-hypertension scenario, the baseline's best at \qty{4.3}{\percent}, improves to \qty{3.3}{\percent}. Reflection is not uniformly beneficial: on its own it leaves the unconstrained scenario worse than the baseline (\qty{10.8}{\percent} against \qty{8.1}{\percent}), and on top of the totaller it worsens the dyslipidemia-obesity scenario from \qty{3.2}{\percent} to \qty{5.1}{\percent}. A critic revising a plan that was already numerically correct has room to make it wrong again.

On the qualitative dimension no judge separates this variant from the baseline.

\section{Model comparison}
\label{sec:results-model-comparison}

Experiment~2 holds the system at the full variant and varies the model and its reasoning effort (\Cref{tab:results-model-summary}, \Cref{fig:results-models-mae}, \Cref{fig:results-models-soft}). On accuracy the grid spans more than a factor of twenty, from \qty{1.08}{\percent} to \qty{24.47}{\percent} -- far wider than the component ablation produced -- so the choice of generating model and its effort matters more than which grounding mechanisms are switched on.

Accuracy alone identifies the wrong winner. Two configurations tie for the lowest error at \qty{1.08}{\percent}: \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} at high effort and \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at its highest. Requiring both dimensions at once narrows the field. \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} at medium effort comes within \qty{0.45}{\percent} of the best error at \qty{1.53}{\percent} while earning the joint-highest soft score from Judge~1 (\num{0.822}) and the second-highest from Judge~2 (\num{0.839}), at \qty{81}{\second} per plan; no configuration beats it on both dimensions simultaneously (\Cref{fig:results-tradeoff-mean}). At the other end, \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at its provider default is last on both, at \qty{24.47}{\percent} and \num{0.694}.

Collapsing the effort levels (\Cref{fig:results-models-by-model,fig:results-models-soft-by-model}) gives each base model a single pair of figures. \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} leads on both dimensions (\qty{1.87}{\percent} and \num{0.808}) for \qty{86}{\second} a plan. \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} and \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} are indistinguishable on error (\qty{2.66}{\percent} against \qty{2.64}{\percent}), but preference separates them, at \num{0.807} against \num{0.791}, as does latency, at \qty{95}{\second} against \qty{147}{\second}. \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} trails on both (\qty{13.39}{\percent} and \num{0.730}), though it is between two and four times faster than anything else, which \Cref{sec:results-effort} returns to.

The iteration counts in \Cref{tab:results-model-summary} are easy to misread. The critic runs on the same model as the generator it reviews (\Cref{sec:design-agents}), so the count reflects not the plans but how demanding that model is of itself. \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} averages between \num{1.33} and \num{1.67} refinement passes, whereas \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} averages \num{0.02} -- it approves its own first draft almost every time. The totaller-call column points the other way: \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} invokes the totaller \num{3.69} times per run, the most of any base model, while \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} invokes it the least, at \num{2.56}. The model that almost never enters its own reflection loop had already checked its arithmetic deterministically before the critic saw the draft, yet beats \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} on both dimensions. A low iteration count is therefore not a quality signal: it may mark a more permissive self-critic, or one that has already done the grounding work the loop would otherwise do.

\section{Reasoning effort}
\label{sec:results-effort}

This section re-cuts Experiment~2 by effort. On accuracy, effort produces the most regular trend in this evaluation. Pooled into three buckets (\Cref{fig:results-effort-mae}), mean macro error falls from \qty{8.66}{\percent} at low effort to \qty{4.12}{\percent} at medium and \qty{2.64}{\percent} at high, and the same monotone ordering holds within each base model -- from \qty{24.47}{\percent} to \qty{6.50}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}, \qty{3.58}{\percent} to \qty{1.88}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}, \qty{2.99}{\percent} to \qty{1.08}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} and \qty{3.59}{\percent} to \qty{1.08}{\percent} for \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}. Longer deliberation reliably improves how closely plans hit a numeric target, consistently enough to survive averaging across four vendors' incomparable effort scales.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/generated/models/mae_pct_by_effort.pdf}
    \caption{Macro error by reasoning-effort bucket, across all four base models. Error bars: standard error of the mean.}
    \label{fig:results-effort-mae}
\end{figure}

Preference does not follow (\Cref{fig:results-effort-soft}). Almost all of the qualitative movement happens in the first step: Judge~1's soft score rises from \num{0.770} to \num{0.794} between low and medium effort, then falls back to \num{0.787} at high, while Judge~2 records \num{0.801}, \num{0.823} and \num{0.831}. The judges disagree about the sign of the final step, and both verdicts on it are smaller than the run-to-run spread of \Cref{sec:results-stability}, so the defensible reading is that preference satisfaction improves from low to medium effort and is flat thereafter. Within individual models the pattern is sharper: the three stronger models peak at their middle setting and lose ground at their highest -- \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} most visibly, from \num{0.802} to \num{0.774} -- and only \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} improves at every step.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/generated/models/soft_aggregate_by_effort.pdf}
    \caption{Soft score by reasoning-effort bucket, both judges.}
    \label{fig:results-effort-soft}
\end{figure}

Effort beyond the middle setting therefore buys accuracy and nothing else, while reliably costing time. Mean elapsed time rises from \qty{52.0}{\second} to \qty{76.6}{\second} to \qty{142.6}{\second} across the three buckets (\Cref{fig:results-effort-elapsed}), and the extremes span wider still: \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort completes a plan in \qty{21.2}{\second}, \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at its highest takes \qty{276.8}{\second}. \ref{req:interactive-runtime}'s interactive-session requirement is met at the low end and not the top, making the top bucket defensible only where the numeric target is what matters and the wait is acceptable.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/generated/models/elapsed_s_by_effort.pdf}
    \caption{Wall-clock time per run by reasoning-effort bucket. Error bars: standard error of the mean.}
    \label{fig:results-effort-elapsed}
\end{figure}

H3~asks how far effort can stand in for model capability; both dimensions answer the same way: substantially, but not enough. Raising \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} from default to high effort is the single largest improvement any configuration change produces, cutting error from \qty{24.47}{\percent} to \qty{6.50}{\percent} and lifting its Judge~1 soft score from \num{0.694} to \num{0.765} -- recovering roughly three quarters of its accuracy deficit and half of its preference deficit against the best configuration in the grid.

It is not enough to make the substitution a good decision. \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} at its \emph{lowest} effort reaches \qty{2.99}{\percent} and \num{0.788}, beating the effort-boosted weak model on both dimensions, in \qty{58}{\second} against \qty{44}{\second}. Those \qty{14}{\second} buy more than the entire effort escalation did. Substitution works in the direction H3~proposes, but only to rescue a model one is already committed to; where the model is a free choice, spending the latency budget on capability dominates.

\textbf{H3} is supported in all three parts, with that qualification: plan quality varies with model and effort, and raising the weakest model's effort recovers most of its deficit -- but effort is best understood as a way to rescue a model fixed by other constraints, not as an alternative to choosing a capable one.

\section{Reflection behaviour and computational overhead}
\label{sec:results-overhead}

\Cref{fig:results-ablation-elapsed} quantifies what the two mechanisms cost in elapsed time (\Cref{def:elapsed-time}). The totaller is cheap: it lifts a run from \qty{29.8}{\second} to \qty{39.2}{\second}, about a third, for the fourfold error reduction of \Cref{sec:results-ablation}. Reflection is expensive: it takes the same run past \qty{100}{\second} whether or not the totaller is enabled, roughly tripling the baseline, and adds nothing on either dimension once the totaller is present. Tokens tell the same story (\Cref{fig:results-ablation-tokens}, \Cref{def:token-spend}): the totaller lifts mean spend from about \num{35000} to \num{66000}, almost entirely in the cache-read share as each check resends the candidate plan, and reflection takes it to about \num{127000}, since every critic and refiner pass repeats a large share of the context.

\Cref{fig:results-ablation-iterations} shows where reflection's time goes. The loop runs \num{1.93} iterations on average without the totaller and \num{1.67} with it (\Cref{def:iterations}), against a cap of three. The reduction is small but points the right way: a critic holding deterministic totals converges slightly sooner than one that must judge numeric adequacy for itself -- the grounding effect of \Cref{sec:design-reflection} showing up in the loop's behaviour, not in the plan.

\Cref{fig:results-ablation-totaller-calls} records how often the model itself invoked the totaller (\Cref{def:totaller-calls}): \num{1.47} calls for the totaller-only variant, \num{2.80} for the full variant. The critique-and-refine loop nearly doubles how often a plan is checked deterministically, because the refiner re-verifies on each pass -- part of why reflection roughly doubles input tokens while buying no accuracy the totaller had not already delivered.

    \centering

\textbf{H2} is unsupported: neither judge detects a soft-score or safety-score improvement from reflection, and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows any such effect would be smaller than the run-to-run noise. Added to the totaller the loop moves macro error by less than the standard error of either mean, at roughly triple the wall-clock time. What remains in its favour -- its own behaviour and what individual critiques visibly correct -- the aggregate metrics do not reward.

\section{Stability and judge agreement}
\label{sec:results-stability}

Every mean here averages over repetitions of a nondeterministic process, so how much an individual run deviates from its configuration's mean bounds the weight the reported differences can carry. \Cref{fig:results-stability} reports the cell spread and run-to-run variability of \Cref{def:spread,def:variability}. Pooled over every cell of both grids, the soft-score spread is \num{0.026} under Judge~1 and \num{0.030} under Judge~2. Both are comfortably smaller than the roughly \num{0.13} gap between the best and worst configurations, so the qualitative ordering is not a sampling artefact; but they equal or exceed the gaps between the four ablation variants, a quantitative reason not to read into that ordering.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/generated/stability/run_variability.pdf}
    \caption{Run-to-run variability of the soft score (\Cref{def:spread}), by model and pooled, both judges.}
    \label{fig:results-stability}
\end{figure}

The two judges behave as \Cref{sec:impl-judges} intended. Judge~2 scores higher than Judge~1 almost everywhere -- by roughly \numrange{0.01}{0.08} -- so absolute soft-score values are judge-dependent and should never be quoted as properties of the plans alone. Their \emph{rankings} largely agree: both place \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort last by a wide margin, both rank its three configurations in increasing order of effort, and both fail to separate the ablation variants. The one visible disagreement is instructive: Judge~1 ranks \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at its highest effort last of that model's three, at \num{0.774}, whereas Judge~2 places it comfortably above the low-effort one, at \num{0.829}. The judge sharing a family with this model is thus harsher on it, the opposite of the self-preference bias the design guarded against; the family overlap did not inflate that model's scores.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:results-threats}
\label{sec:conclusions-limitations}

Several limitations bound how far these results generalise.

The scenario set is five profiles, chosen to vary in what they demand -- an unconstrained day, a hard exclusion against a conflicting request, interacting chronic conditions -- but too few to support claims about dietary planning in general, and the high safety scores may reflect that none is adversarial in the way a determined user could be. More scenarios would have exceeded the grid budget.

Both judges are language models. The second judge makes vendor-specific bias visible and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows it did not distort the rankings, but a bias shared by both -- a common preference for longer rationales, say -- would remain invisible from inside this evaluation. No human expert reviewed any plan, so every qualitative number is a proxy for the judgement that would actually matter.

Reasoning-effort levels are vendor-defined labels, not calibrated quantities. Within-model effort comparisons are sound, since only the label changes; the pooled cross-model comparison of \Cref{sec:results-effort} assumes a correspondence between vendors' scales that no provider guarantees, and should be read as a direction, not a measurement.

The system produces a single, non-interactive plan per session; nothing here speaks to a multi-turn, incrementally edited planning conversation.

Finally, this evaluation is one snapshot of a fast-moving set of hosted models, any of which may be silently updated behind the same identifier. The architectural conclusions -- that deterministic grounding is cheap and effective, that self-critique is expensive and bought nothing the grounding had not already delivered, that effort settings deserve tuning -- are more durable than the numbers attached to any named model.

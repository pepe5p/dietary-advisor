\chapter{Results and discussion}
\label{cha:results}

This chapter reports what the two experiments defined in \Cref{cha:methodology} measured, and what those measurements do and do not license as conclusions. Three results carry most of the argument. The deterministic totaller reduces macronutrient error by nearly half on its own, for about a third more run time. The critique-and-refine loop reduces it further, but by a smaller margin and at roughly triple the run time, and it moves neither judge's qualitative scores. In the model comparison, raising a weak model's reasoning-effort level recovers most of the gap to models several times slower, while the same adjustment does little for the models that were already strong -- the substitution effect H3~predicts, though only in one direction. The chapter then reports what the two judges revealed about the reliability of its own qualitative measurements, and closes with the limits that should be attached to all of the above.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:results-setup}

The component ablation experiment was run with \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} as the fixed language model across all four variants of \Cref{tab:methodology-variants}, against each of the five scenarios of \Cref{tab:methodology-scenarios}, three times per cell, for 60 runs. The model comparison experiment fixed the system at the full variant and swept the twelve model-and-effort configurations described in \Cref{sec:methodology-model-grid}: \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}, \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}, \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} and \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}, each at three effort levels, again against all five scenarios three times, for 180 runs. Both grids are complete; every cell reported below rests on 15 runs.

Every plan was scored by both judges of \Cref{sec:methodology-qualitative}, three times each: \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} as Judge~1 and \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash-0731} as Judge~2. Judge~1 therefore shares a model family with one of the twelve compared configurations, which is the overlap the second judge exists to expose rather than an oversight; \Cref{sec:results-stability} reports whether it distorted anything. Judge-independent metrics -- macronutrient error, iteration count and elapsed time -- are identical whichever judge's records they are read from, and are quoted without a judge attribution throughout.

Two conventions apply to every number and figure below. A figure quoted for a variant, a model or an effort level is an arithmetic mean over the runs in that group. Error bars on the figures show the standard error of that mean, since what the figures are used to decide is whether two group means differ; where the variation between individual runs is the point instead, the population standard deviation is quoted in the text.

\section{Component ablation}
\label{sec:results-ablation}

\Cref{tab:results-ablation-summary} reports the four-variant comparison and \Cref{fig:results-ablation-mae} shows its headline column. Enabling the totaller alone reduces macronutrient MAE from \qty{27.41}{\percent} to \qty{14.13}{\percent}, very nearly halving it. Enabling the reflection loop alone reaches \qty{15.96}{\percent} -- a real improvement, but a smaller one than the totaller achieves, and bought at roughly three times the wall-clock time. Combining both gives the lowest error of the four, \qty{12.43}{\percent} MAE and an MSE of \num{611} against \num{815} for the totaller alone, which indicates that the two mechanisms are partly complementary rather than one subsuming the other.

The variation between runs matters as much as the mean here. The baseline's population standard deviation is \qty{20.11}{\percent}, larger than the entire mean error of any grounded variant, while the three grounded variants sit between \qty{4.84}{\percent} and \qty{7.71}{\percent}. An ungrounded plan is therefore not merely worse on average but substantially less predictable, which for a system whose failures are silent is arguably the more serious property. \Cref{sec:results-error-analysis} traces most of that deviation to a single scenario.

The qualitative side separates the variants far less, and \Cref{fig:results-ablation-soft} shows what the second judge contributes. Both judges place all four variants within a narrow band -- roughly \numrange{0.79}{0.81} for Judge~1 and \numrange{0.80}{0.84} for Judge~2 -- so neither reflects the two-fold difference in macronutrient accuracy visible in \Cref{fig:results-ablation-mae}. What the two series also show is a consistent offset: Judge~2 rates essentially every variant higher than Judge~1 does, by a margin comparable to the differences between the variants themselves. That is the central caution of this chapter's qualitative results, and \Cref{sec:results-stability} takes it up properly.

\Cref{tab:results-per-scenario} breaks the MAE figures down by scenario. The benefit of grounding is real but uneven. The diabetes-hypertension scenario is the baseline's best case at \qty{7.1}{\percent} and gains nothing from either mechanism -- both grounded variants are slightly worse -- whereas the lactose-intolerant-athlete scenario falls from \qty{53.4}{\percent} to \qty{13.5}{\percent} once the totaller is available. Grounding helps most exactly where the unaided model was doing worst.

\section{Model comparison}
\label{sec:results-model-comparison}

\Cref{tab:results-model-summary} lists all twelve configurations and \Cref{fig:results-models-mae} plots their macronutrient error. The best configuration in the entire grid is \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} at low effort, at \qty{8.05}{\percent} MAE, and the worst by a wide margin is \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at its provider default, at \qty{27.61}{\percent} -- a range of more than a factor of three, wider than the range the entire component ablation produced. The choice of generating model and how hard it is allowed to think therefore matters at least as much as which grounding mechanisms are switched on.

Collapsing the effort levels, as \Cref{fig:results-models-by-model} does, gives each base model a single figure: \qty{9.60}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}, \qty{12.18}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash}, \qty{13.51}{\percent} for \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} and \qty{17.93}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}. And the ordering by macronutrient accuracy does not match the ordering by qualitative score: \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} earns the highest soft aggregate from both judges while placing second on error, which is a reminder that the two metrics measure genuinely different things.

The iteration counts in \Cref{tab:results-model-summary} expose a mechanism worth naming, because it is easy to misread. The critic runs on the same model as the generator it reviews (\Cref{sec:design-agents}), so the iteration count is not a property of the plans alone but of how demanding that particular model is of itself. \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} averages between \num{1.33} and \num{1.67} refinement passes, whereas \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} averages \num{0.02} -- it approves its own first draft almost every time. Yet \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} still achieves a lower MAE than \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}. A model that rarely invokes its own reflection loop is therefore not necessarily producing worse plans; it may simply be a more permissive critic of itself, which is a caveat for anyone tempted to read iteration counts as a quality signal.

\section{Reasoning effort}
\label{sec:results-effort}

Pooling the twelve configurations into three effort buckets produces the one monotonic trend in this evaluation that holds across every step, shown in \Cref{fig:results-effort-mae}: mean MAE falls from \qty{16.36}{\percent} at low effort to \qty{12.45}{\percent} at medium and \qty{11.11}{\percent} at high. Both judges' soft aggregates move in the same direction, from \num{0.770} to \num{0.787} for Judge~1 and from \num{0.800} to \num{0.831} for Judge~2. Reasoning effort buys accuracy, and it does so consistently enough to survive being averaged across four vendors' incomparable effort scales.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/generated/models/mae_pct_by_effort.pdf}
    \caption{Macronutrient MAE aggregated by reasoning-effort bucket across all four base models.}
    \label{fig:results-effort-mae}
\end{figure}

The aggregate conceals how unevenly that benefit is distributed. For \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}, raising effort from the provider default to high cuts MAE from \qty{27.61}{\percent} to \qty{9.48}{\percent}, a reduction of roughly two thirds, and lifts its Judge~1 soft aggregate from \num{0.694} to \num{0.765}. That single configuration change moves the highest-error model in the comparison to within \qty{1.5}{\percent} of the lowest-error configuration, at a fraction of the larger models' latency. For every other model the adjustment changes little or works against accuracy: \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} records its lowest error at its \emph{lowest} effort (\qty{8.05}{\percent}, against \qty{10.66}{\percent} at high), \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} records its lowest in the middle (\qty{10.55}{\percent}), and only \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} improves monotonically, from \qty{16.48}{\percent} to \qty{11.61}{\percent}. The monotone aggregate trend, in other words, is carried largely by one model whose default setting left it under-provisioned.

What effort consistently does buy is time. \Cref{fig:results-effort-elapsed} shows mean elapsed time rising from \qty{52.0}{\second} to \qty{76.6}{\second} to \qty{142.6}{\second} across the three buckets, and the individual extremes span a wider range: \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort completes a plan in \qty{21.2}{\second}, while \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at its highest setting takes \qty{276.8}{\second}, thirteen times longer for a higher macronutrient error. Latency therefore rises with effort at every step, which accuracy does not, and NFR4's requirement of an interactive session is met at the low end of this range and not at the top.

\section{Reflection behaviour and computational overhead}
\label{sec:results-overhead}

The two mechanisms under ablation are not free, and \Cref{fig:results-ablation-elapsed} quantifies what they cost in time. The totaller is cheap: it lifts a run from \qty{29.8}{\second} to \qty{39.2}{\second}, about a third more, in exchange for halving macronutrient error. Reflection is expensive: it takes the same run past \qty{100}{\second} whether or not the totaller is also enabled, roughly tripling the baseline. Token telemetry tells the same story from the other side. Enabling the totaller raises mean input tokens per run from about \num{32000} to \num{61000}, because each check resends the full candidate plan, and enabling reflection on top of that roughly doubles it again to about \num{115000}, with requests per run rising from \num{3.5} to \num{11.7}, since every critic pass and every refiner pass repeats a large share of the context.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/generated/ablation/elapsed_s.pdf}
    \caption{Wall-clock time per run by ablation variant. The totaller is inexpensive; reflection is not.}
    \label{fig:results-ablation-elapsed}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:results-ablation-iterations} shows where reflection's time goes. With reflection enabled the loop runs \num{1.93} refinement passes on average without the totaller and \num{1.67} with it, against a cap of three. The reduction when the totaller is present is small but points the right way: a critic holding deterministic totals converges slightly sooner than one that has to judge numeric adequacy for itself, which is the grounding effect described in \Cref{sec:design-reflection} showing up in the loop's own behaviour rather than in the plan.

\section{Stability and judge agreement}
\label{sec:results-stability}

Every mean in this chapter is an average over three repetitions of a nondeterministic process, so the question of how much an individual run deviates from its configuration's mean bears directly on how much weight the differences above can carry. \Cref{fig:results-stability} answers it with the run-to-run variability metric of \Cref{sec:methodology-stability}. Pooled over all 75 cells, the spread of the soft aggregate is \num{0.026} under Judge~1 and \num{0.030} under Judge~2. Both are comfortably smaller than the roughly \num{0.13} gap separating the best and worst configurations' soft aggregates, so the qualitative ordering is not an artefact of sampling; but they are the same size as, or larger than, the gaps between the four ablation variants, which is a quantitative reason not to read anything into that ordering.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/generated/stability/run_variability.pdf}
    \caption{Run-to-run variability of the soft-preference aggregate: the per-cell spread of \Cref{sec:methodology-stability}, averaged per model and pooled, for both judges.}
    \label{fig:results-stability}
\end{figure}

The two judges themselves behave as \Cref{sec:impl-judges} hoped they would. Judge~2 scores higher than Judge~1 almost everywhere -- by roughly \numrange{0.01}{0.08} depending on the configuration -- so absolute soft-aggregate values are clearly judge-dependent and should never be quoted as if they were properties of the plans alone. Their \emph{rankings}, however, largely agree: both place \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort last by a wide margin, both rank the three \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} configurations in increasing order of effort, and both fail to separate the four ablation variants. The one visible disagreement is instructive: Judge~1 ranks \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at its highest effort last of that model's three configurations, at \num{0.774}, whereas Judge~2 places the same configuration comfortably above the low-effort one, at \num{0.829}. The judge that shares a family with this model is therefore, if anything, harsher on it than the independent judge -- the opposite of the self-preference bias the design was guarding against. On the evidence available, the family overlap did not inflate that model's scores.

\section{Error analysis}
\label{sec:results-error-analysis}

Three patterns merit closer examination than the summary statistics allow.

\textbf{The baseline's worst case is one dominant outlier.} The lactose-intolerant-athlete scenario's baseline MAE of \qty{53.4}{\percent} in \Cref{tab:results-per-scenario} is driven overwhelmingly by a single repetition in which the ungrounded model, fitting a calorie-dense dessert into an athlete's already high energy target with nothing checking the result, overshot its fibre target by more than \qty{170}{\percent}; the other two repetitions of that cell are far closer to target. This is precisely the failure a deterministic totaller is designed to catch, and its contribution to the baseline's \qty{20.11}{\percent} standard deviation is why an ungrounded system should be described as unpredictable rather than merely inaccurate.

\textbf{Fibre dominates the residual error even in grounded variants.} The per-nutrient breakdown recorded with each run (\Cref{sec:methodology-quantitative}) shows fibre contributing a disproportionate share of what error remains after grounding, well above energy, protein, carbohydrate or fat. This follows directly from the partial nutrient coverage of \Cref{sec:impl-missing-nutrients}: fibre is among the fields most often missing from a crowd-sourced product record, so for a meaningful share of plans the totaller is exactly summing an incomplete picture. No amount of arithmetic rigour can recover a value the database never recorded, which places a floor on what grounding alone can achieve.

\textbf{Safety adherence is high but no longer uniformly perfect, and the judges disagree about where it slips.} Judge~1 records perfect adherence across all four ablation variants; Judge~2 does not, dropping to \num{0.928} on the totaller-only variant and \num{0.978} on the full variant. In the model comparison both judges flag \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort as the weakest configuration for safety (\num{0.966} and \num{0.963} respectively), which is consistent with it being the weakest configuration on every other axis. But the two disagree about the rest: Judge~1 finds only two further configurations short of perfect, Judge~2 finds five, and only three of those overlap. Since a safety judgement is a claim about whether a specific allergen or diet-pattern rule was broken -- something that ought to be objectively checkable from the plan -- this disagreement is harder to discount than the offset in soft-preference scores. It suggests that allergen compliance is currently being assessed at a level of reliability well below what a deployed system would require, and argues for moving hard exclusions out of the judge's hands and into a deterministic checker, in the same spirit as the no-invented-foods contract.

\section{Discussion of the hypotheses}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

\textbf{H1}~(the deterministic totaller reduces macronutrient error) is supported. Enabling the totaller, with the model and every other flag fixed, cuts MAE from \qty{27.41}{\percent} to \qty{14.13}{\percent}, and the improvement holds in four of the five scenarios rather than resting on one. It is also the least expensive of the two mechanisms in time, adding about a third to run time against the roughly threefold increase reflection brings.

\textbf{H2}~(the critique-and-refine loop improves accuracy and adherence) is supported on accuracy and unsupported on adherence. Reflection does reduce macronutrient error, both alone (\qty{15.96}{\percent}) and on top of the totaller (\qty{12.43}{\percent} against \qty{14.13}{\percent}), but that marginal \qty{1.70}{\percent} comes with roughly triple the wall-clock time and more than triple the requests. On the adherence side the hypothesis fails within this scenario set: neither judge detects a soft-preference or safety improvement from enabling reflection, and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows that any such effect would be smaller than the run-to-run noise in any case. Reflection is defensible on accuracy grounds and on the strength of what it corrects in individual plans, but not on the evidence of the aggregate qualitative metrics.

\textbf{H3}~(plan quality depends on both model choice and reasoning-effort level, which are partly interchangeable) is supported in both of its parts. Model choice matters: aggregated over effort levels, mean MAE ranges from \qty{9.60}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} to \qty{17.93}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}. Effort level matters too, and pooled across models it moves MAE monotonically from \qty{16.36}{\percent} to \qty{11.11}{\percent}. The substitution claim is the more useful half, and the data support it asymmetrically. Raising \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} from its default effort to high cuts its MAE from \qty{27.61}{\percent} to \qty{9.48}{\percent}, which brings the weakest model in the comparison to within \qty{1.5}{\percent} of the best configuration in the grid while remaining several times faster than the models it now matches. The reverse substitution does not hold: for the three stronger models, additional effort is flat or mildly counterproductive, with \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} at its lowest effort outperforming its own high-effort setting. Effort is therefore worth spending where a model's default leaves it under-provisioned, and worth little elsewhere -- for this task, matching the effort level to the model is a more productive adjustment than reaching for a larger model.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:results-threats}

Several limitations bound how far these results generalise.

The scenario set is five profiles. They were chosen to vary in what they demand -- an unconstrained day, a hard exclusion against a conflicting request, interacting chronic conditions -- but five is too few to support claims about dietary planning in general, and the high safety-adherence scores may reflect that none of these scenarios is adversarial in the way a determined user could be.

Both judges are language models. Adding the second judge makes vendor-specific bias visible and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows it did not distort the rankings, but a bias shared by both -- a common preference for longer rationales, say -- would remain invisible from inside this evaluation. No human expert reviewed any plan, so every qualitative number in this chapter is a proxy for the judgement that would actually matter.

Reasoning-effort levels are vendor-defined labels, not calibrated quantities. Comparisons of effort within a single model are sound, since only the label changes; the pooled comparison across models in \Cref{sec:results-effort} assumes a correspondence between different vendors' scales that no provider guarantees, and should be read as a direction rather than a measurement.

Finally, all of the above describes one snapshot of a fast-moving set of hosted models, several of which were released within months of this evaluation and any of which may be silently updated behind the same identifier. The architectural conclusions -- that deterministic grounding is cheap and effective, that self-critique is expensive and marginal, that effort settings deserve tuning -- are more durable than the specific numbers attached to any named model here.

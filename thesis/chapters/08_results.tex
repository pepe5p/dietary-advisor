\chapter{Results and discussion}
\label{cha:results}

This chapter reports what the two experiments defined in \Cref{cha:methodology} measured, and what those measurements do and do not license as conclusions. Read in order, the results tell a story with one clear finding, one qualified one and one surprise. The clear finding is that deterministic grounding works, and works cheaply: a calculator the model can consult halves macronutrient error on its own. The qualified one is that the critique-and-refine loop adds a further, smaller improvement for a large increase in time and requests. The surprise is in the model comparison, where allowing a weak model to reason harder closes most of the gap to models several times its size, while the same lever does almost nothing for the strong models -- which reframes the capability question that H3 asks. The chapter closes with what the two judges revealed about the reliability of its own qualitative measurements, and with the limits that should be attached to all of the above.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:results-setup}

The component ablation experiment was run with \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} as the fixed language model across all four variants of \Cref{tab:methodology-variants}, against each of the five scenarios of \Cref{tab:methodology-scenarios}, three times per cell, for 60 runs. The model comparison experiment fixed the system at the full variant and swept the twelve model-and-effort configurations described in \Cref{sec:methodology-model-grid}: \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}, \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}, \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} and \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}, each at three effort levels, again against all five scenarios three times, for 180 runs. Both grids are complete; every cell reported below rests on 15 runs.

Every plan was scored by both judges of \Cref{sec:methodology-qualitative}, three times each: \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} as Judge~1 and \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash-0731} as Judge~2. Judge~1 therefore shares a model family with one of the twelve compared configurations, which is the overlap the second judge exists to expose rather than an oversight; \Cref{sec:results-stability} reports whether it distorted anything. Judge-independent metrics -- macronutrient error, iteration count and elapsed time -- are identical whichever judge's records they are read from, and are quoted without a judge attribution throughout.

\section{Component ablation}
\label{sec:results-ablation}

\Cref{tab:results-ablation-summary} reports the four-variant comparison and \Cref{fig:results-ablation-mae} shows its headline column. Enabling the totaller alone reduces macronutrient MAE from \qty{27.41}{\percent} to \qty{14.13}{\percent}, very nearly halving it. Enabling the reflection loop alone reaches \qty{15.96}{\percent} -- a real improvement, but a smaller one than the totaller achieves, and bought at roughly three times the wall-clock time. Combining both gives the best result of the four, \qty{12.43}{\percent} MAE and by some distance the lowest MSE at \num{611}, confirming that the two mechanisms are partly complementary rather than one subsuming the other.

The spread matters as much as the mean here. The baseline's population standard deviation is \qty{20.11}{\percent}, larger than the entire mean error of any grounded variant, while the three grounded variants sit between \qty{4.84}{\percent} and \qty{7.71}{\percent}. An ungrounded plan is therefore not merely worse on average but substantially less predictable, which for a system whose failures are silent is arguably the more serious property. \Cref{sec:results-error-analysis} traces most of that spread to a single scenario.

The picture from the qualitative side is far less dramatic, and \Cref{fig:results-ablation-soft} shows why the second judge is worth its cost. Both judges place all four variants within a narrow band -- roughly \numrange{0.79}{0.81} for Judge~1 and \numrange{0.80}{0.84} for Judge~2 -- so neither detects the two-fold difference in macronutrient accuracy that \Cref{fig:results-ablation-mae} makes obvious. What the two series also show is a consistent offset: Judge~2 rates essentially every variant higher than Judge~1 does, by a margin comparable to the differences between the variants themselves. That is the central caution of this chapter's qualitative results, and \Cref{sec:results-stability} takes it up properly.

\Cref{tab:results-per-scenario} breaks the MAE figures down by scenario. The benefit of grounding is real but uneven. The diabetes-hypertension scenario is the baseline's best case at \qty{7.1}{\percent} and gains nothing from either mechanism -- both grounded variants are slightly worse -- whereas the lactose-intolerant-athlete scenario falls from \qty{53.4}{\percent} to \qty{13.5}{\percent} once the totaller is available. Grounding helps most exactly where the unaided model was doing worst.

\section{Model comparison}
\label{sec:results-model-comparison}

\Cref{tab:results-model-summary} lists all twelve configurations and \Cref{fig:results-models-mae} plots their macronutrient error. The best configuration in the entire grid is \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} at low effort, at \qty{8.05}{\percent} MAE, and the worst by a wide margin is \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at its provider default, at \qty{27.61}{\percent} -- a spread of more than a factor of three, which is larger than the spread the entire component ablation produced. The choice of generating model and how hard it is allowed to think therefore matters at least as much as which grounding mechanisms are switched on.

Collapsing the effort levels, as \Cref{fig:results-models-by-model} does, gives each base model a single figure: \qty{9.60}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}, \qty{12.18}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash}, \qty{13.51}{\percent} for \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} and \qty{17.93}{\percent} for \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}. Two observations follow. The newer \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} does not outperform the older \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} on this task, so general model progress does not automatically transfer to constrained numeric planning. And the ordering by macronutrient accuracy does not match the ordering by qualitative score: \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} earns the highest soft aggregate from both judges while placing second on error, which is a reminder that the two metrics measure genuinely different things.

The iteration counts in \Cref{tab:results-model-summary} expose a mechanism worth naming, because it is easy to misread. The critic runs on the same model as the generator it reviews (\Cref{sec:design-agents}), so the iteration count is not a property of the plans alone but of how demanding that particular model is of itself. \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} averages between \num{1.33} and \num{1.67} refinement passes, whereas \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} averages \num{0.02} -- it approves its own first draft almost every time. Yet \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} still achieves a lower MAE than \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}. A model that rarely invokes its own reflection loop is therefore not necessarily producing worse plans; it may simply be a more permissive critic of itself, which is a caveat for anyone tempted to read iteration counts as a quality signal.

\section{Reasoning effort}
\label{sec:results-effort}

Pooling the twelve configurations into three effort buckets produces the cleanest monotonic trend in the entire evaluation, shown in \Cref{fig:results-effort-mae}: mean MAE falls from \qty{16.36}{\percent} at low effort to \qty{12.45}{\percent} at medium and \qty{11.11}{\percent} at high. Both judges' soft aggregates move in the same direction, from \num{0.770} to \num{0.787} for Judge~1 and from \num{0.800} to \num{0.831} for Judge~2. Reasoning effort buys accuracy, and it does so consistently enough to survive being averaged across four vendors' incomparable effort scales.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../outputs/figures/models/mae_pct_by_effort.pdf}
    \caption{Macronutrient MAE aggregated by reasoning-effort bucket across all four base models.}
    \label{fig:results-effort-mae}
\end{figure}

The aggregate hides the more interesting result, which is how unevenly that benefit is distributed. For \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}, raising effort from the provider default to high cuts MAE from \qty{27.61}{\percent} to \qty{9.48}{\percent}, a reduction of roughly two thirds, and lifts its Judge~1 soft aggregate from \num{0.694} to \num{0.765}. That single configuration change takes the weakest model in the comparison from worst-in-grid to within striking distance of the best, at a fraction of the larger models' latency. For every other model the lever is nearly inert or actively unhelpful: \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} is best at its \emph{lowest} effort (\qty{8.05}{\percent}, against \qty{10.66}{\percent} at high), \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} is best in the middle, and only \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} improves monotonically, and then only from \qty{16.48}{\percent} to \qty{11.61}{\percent}. The monotone aggregate trend, in other words, is carried almost entirely by one model that started out badly configured.

What effort reliably does buy is time. \Cref{fig:results-effort-elapsed} shows mean elapsed time rising from \qty{52.0}{\second} to \qty{76.6}{\second} to \qty{142.6}{\second} across the three buckets, and the extremes are starker still: \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort completes a plan in \qty{21.2}{\second}, while \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at its highest setting takes \qty{276.8}{\second}, more than thirteen times longer for a worse macronutrient error. Latency scales with effort far more dependably than accuracy does, and NFR4's requirement of an interactive session is comfortably met at the low end of this range and clearly violated at the top.

\section{Reflection behaviour and computational overhead}
\label{sec:results-overhead}

The two mechanisms under ablation are not free, and \Cref{fig:results-ablation-elapsed} quantifies what they cost in time. The totaller is cheap: it lifts a run from \qty{29.8}{\second} to \qty{39.2}{\second}, about a third more, in exchange for halving macronutrient error. Reflection is expensive: it takes the same run past \qty{100}{\second} whether or not the totaller is also enabled, roughly tripling the baseline. Token telemetry tells the same story from the other side. Enabling the totaller raises mean input tokens per run from about \num{32000} to \num{61000}, because each check resends the full candidate plan, and enabling reflection on top of that roughly doubles it again to about \num{115000}, with requests per run rising from \num{3.5} to \num{11.7}, since every critic pass and every refiner pass repeats a large share of the context.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../outputs/figures/ablation/elapsed_s.pdf}
    \caption{Wall-clock time per run by ablation variant. The totaller is inexpensive; reflection is not.}
    \label{fig:results-ablation-elapsed}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:results-ablation-iterations} shows where reflection's time goes. With reflection enabled the loop runs \num{1.93} refinement passes on average without the totaller and \num{1.67} with it, against a cap of three. The reduction when the totaller is present is small but points the right way: a critic holding deterministic totals converges slightly sooner than one that has to judge numeric adequacy for itself, which is the grounding effect described in \Cref{sec:design-reflection} showing up in the loop's own behaviour rather than in the plan.

\section{Stability and judge agreement}
\label{sec:results-stability}

Every mean in this chapter is an average over three repetitions of a nondeterministic process, so the question of how much an individual run deviates from its configuration's mean bears directly on how much weight the differences above can carry. \Cref{fig:results-stability} answers it with the run-to-run variability metric of \Cref{sec:methodology-stability}. Pooled over all 75 configuration groups, the mean absolute deviation of the soft aggregate is \num{0.026} under Judge~1 and \num{0.030} under Judge~2. Both are comfortably smaller than the roughly \num{0.13} spread separating the best and worst configurations' soft aggregates, so the qualitative ordering is not an artefact of sampling; but they are the same size as, or larger than, the gaps between the four ablation variants, which is a quantitative reason not to read anything into that ordering.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../outputs/figures/stability/run_variability.pdf}
    \caption{Run-to-run variability of the soft-preference aggregate: mean absolute deviation across the three repetitions of each configuration, per model and pooled, for both judges.}
    \label{fig:results-stability}
\end{figure}

The two judges themselves behave as \Cref{sec:impl-judges} hoped they would. Judge~2 scores higher than Judge~1 almost everywhere -- by roughly \numrange{0.01}{0.08} depending on the configuration -- so absolute soft-aggregate values are clearly judge-dependent and should never be quoted as if they were properties of the plans alone. Their \emph{rankings}, however, largely agree: both place \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort last by a wide margin, both rank the three \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} configurations in increasing order of effort, and both fail to separate the four ablation variants. The one visible disagreement is instructive: Judge~1 ranks \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at its highest effort last of that model's three configurations, at \num{0.774}, whereas Judge~2 places the same configuration comfortably above the low-effort one, at \num{0.829}. The judge that shares a family with this model is therefore, if anything, harsher on it than the independent judge -- the opposite of the self-preference bias the design was guarding against. On the evidence available, the family overlap did not inflate that model's scores.

\section{Error analysis}
\label{sec:results-error-analysis}

Three patterns merit closer examination than the summary statistics allow.

\textbf{The baseline's worst case is one dominant outlier.} The lactose-intolerant-athlete scenario's baseline MAE of \qty{53.4}{\percent} in \Cref{tab:results-per-scenario} is driven overwhelmingly by a single repetition in which the ungrounded model, fitting a calorie-dense dessert into an athlete's already high energy target with nothing checking the result, overshot its fibre target by more than \qty{170}{\percent}; the other two repetitions of that cell are far closer to target. This is precisely the failure a deterministic totaller is designed to catch, and its contribution to the baseline's \qty{20.11}{\percent} standard deviation is why an ungrounded system should be described as unpredictable rather than merely inaccurate.

\textbf{Fibre dominates the residual error even in grounded variants.} The per-nutrient breakdown recorded with each run (\Cref{sec:methodology-quantitative}) shows fibre contributing a disproportionate share of what error remains after grounding, well above energy, protein, carbohydrate or fat. This follows directly from the partial nutrient coverage of \Cref{sec:impl-missing-nutrients}: fibre is among the fields most often missing from a crowd-sourced product record, so for a meaningful share of plans the totaller is exactly summing an incomplete picture. No amount of arithmetic rigour can recover a value the database never recorded, which places a floor on what grounding alone can achieve.

\textbf{Safety adherence is high but no longer uniformly perfect, and the judges disagree about where it slips.} Judge~1 records perfect adherence across all four ablation variants; Judge~2 does not, dropping to \num{0.928} on the totaller-only variant and \num{0.978} on the full variant. In the model comparison both judges flag \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} at default effort as the weakest configuration for safety (\num{0.966} and \num{0.963} respectively), which is consistent with it being the weakest configuration on every other axis. But the two disagree about the rest: Judge~1 finds only two further configurations short of perfect, Judge~2 finds five, and only three of those overlap. Since a safety judgement is a claim about whether a specific allergen or diet-pattern rule was broken -- something that ought to be objectively checkable from the plan -- this disagreement is more troubling than the offset in soft-preference scores. It suggests that allergen compliance is currently being assessed at a level of reliability well below what a deployed system would require, and argues for moving hard exclusions out of the judge's hands and into a deterministic checker, in the same spirit as the no-invented-foods contract.

\section{Discussion of the hypotheses}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

\textbf{H1} (grounding in a food knowledge base plus a deterministic totaller reduces macronutrient error) is supported. Enabling the totaller alone, with the model and every other flag fixed, nearly halves MAE relative to the already food-grounded baseline, and the improvement holds in four of five scenarios rather than resting on one. It is also the cheapest intervention measured here, adding about a third to run time.

\textbf{H2} (the critique-and-refine loop further improves accuracy and adherence beyond grounding alone) is partially supported. Reflection does improve macronutrient error, both alone and on top of the totaller, but its marginal contribution over the totaller is modest -- \qty{14.13}{\percent} to \qty{12.43}{\percent} -- while roughly tripling wall-clock time and more than tripling requests. On the adherence side the hypothesis is not supported at all within this scenario set: neither judge detects a soft-preference or safety improvement from enabling reflection, and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows that any such effect would in any case be smaller than the run-to-run noise. Reflection is defensible on accuracy grounds and on the strength of what it corrects in individual plans, but not on the evidence of the aggregate qualitative metrics.

\textbf{H3} (the benefit depends on model capability) is supported, but only once capability is read as this thesis's revised formulation intends -- as a function of both the model and its reasoning effort. The naive version of the hypothesis, that a weaker model gains more from grounding and critique, is not what the data show: the weakest configuration engages the reflection loop rarely and still produces the worst plans. What the data do show is that the weakest model's deficit is largely a configuration artefact rather than a capability ceiling. Given a high reasoning-effort setting, \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} improves by roughly two thirds and approaches the accuracy of models that are far slower and more expensive to run, while the same lever leaves stronger models flat or slightly worse. The benefit of spending more computation therefore accrues overwhelmingly to models that were under-provisioned to begin with, which is a more actionable finding than the original hypothesis anticipated: for this task, matching effort to the model matters more than reaching for a larger model.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:results-threats}

Several limitations bound how far these results generalise.

The scenario set is five profiles. They were chosen to vary in what they demand -- an unconstrained day, a hard exclusion against a conflicting request, interacting chronic conditions -- but five is too few to support claims about dietary planning in general, and the high safety-adherence scores may reflect that none of these scenarios is adversarial in the way a determined user could be.

Both judges are language models. Adding the second judge makes vendor-specific bias visible and \Cref{sec:results-stability} shows it did not distort the rankings, but a bias shared by both -- a common preference for longer rationales, say -- would remain invisible from inside this evaluation. No human expert reviewed any plan, so every qualitative number in this chapter is a proxy for the judgement that would actually matter.

Reasoning-effort levels are vendor-defined labels, not calibrated quantities. Comparisons of effort within a single model are sound, since only the label changes; the pooled comparison across models in \Cref{sec:results-effort} assumes a correspondence between different vendors' scales that no provider guarantees, and should be read as a direction rather than a measurement.

Finally, all of the above describes one snapshot of a fast-moving set of hosted models, several of which were released within months of this evaluation and any of which may be silently updated behind the same identifier. The architectural conclusions -- that deterministic grounding is cheap and effective, that self-critique is expensive and marginal, that effort settings deserve tuning -- are more durable than the specific numbers attached to any named model here.

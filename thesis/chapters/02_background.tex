\chapter{Background and related work}
\label{cha:background}

The design defended in this thesis is a compromise between two research traditions that have each solved half of the dietary planning problem and neither of which has solved the other half. Operations research settled the arithmetic side of the problem in the 1940s and has been refining it ever since, producing diets that are provably optimal and frequently unappetising. Natural language processing has, much more recently, produced systems that understand what a person is asking for and can write a recipe they might enjoy, but that cannot be trusted to add up the recipe's contents. This chapter reviews both traditions and the machinery that has grown up between them: the failure modes that make an unaided language model unsuitable for this task, the tool-use and self-correction techniques that partially mitigate those failures, and the evaluation methods that make claims about such systems measurable. It closes with the recent dietary systems this work is most directly comparable to, and with the specific gap it sets out to fill.

\section{Diet planning as a mathematical optimisation problem}
\label{sec:background-diet-optimisation}

Long before language models existed, dietary planning was already recognised as a problem with a precise mathematical structure. In 1945, the economist George Stigler posed the question of the cheapest combination of 77 foods that would satisfy the recommended daily allowances of nine nutrients for an active man, and solved it by heuristic elimination to within a few cents of the true optimum~\cite{stigler1945cost}. Two years after linear programming and the simplex algorithm were formalised, George Dantzig used Stigler's own data as one of the first real-world test cases for the new method, confirming the heuristic solution and turning the \emph{diet problem} into a standard illustration of linear programming to this day~\cite{dantzig1990diet}. The formulation is simple: nutrient requirements become linear inequality constraints, food quantities become continuous decision variables, and a cost or deviation term becomes the objective to minimise.

Subsequent work refined the formulation rather than the underlying idea. Sklan and Dariel extended the linear programme to mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) so that foods eaten in whole units, and higher-level constraints such as meal structure or exchange-group frequency, could be represented directly~\cite{sklan1993diet}. A 2018 review of fifty-two studies applying linear programming to diet problems found that the overwhelming majority impose only nutritional and cost constraints, with far fewer incorporating environmental or -- crucially -- palatability and acceptability constraints, and concluded that no study had yet offered a satisfactory way to encode how acceptable a mathematically optimal diet actually is to the person who has to eat it~\cite{vandooren2018review}. This is the central limitation of the classical approach: an LP or MILP solver finds the numerically optimal diet with respect to whatever is written into its constraint matrix, but it has no notion of a recipe, a cuisine, or a craving, and encoding those notions as linear constraints is, at best, an approximation of something inherently linguistic and personal.

This gap is precisely where large language models are attractive: they excel at exactly the semantic, preference-laden reasoning that a solver cannot express, while a solver excels at exactly the arithmetic a language model cannot be trusted to perform unaided. A 2026 study combining GPT-4o with a MILP solver over a shared food catalogue makes this trade-off explicit: an LLM alone was rated by domain experts as the least accurate of three approaches on a nutrient-accuracy scale, a MILP solver alone was the most numerically accurate but the least personalised and among the least practical, and a pipeline in which the LLM filters candidate foods for the solver and then narrates the solver's output scored close to the MILP-only baseline on accuracy while matching or exceeding the LLM-only baseline on personalisation and practicality~\cite{alkeyeva2026chatgpt}. How this thesis splits the same labour without a general-purpose solver is a technology choice, taken up in \Cref{sec:tools-aggregation}.

\section{Failure modes of large language models in numeric and safety-critical tasks}
\label{sec:background-llm-failure-modes}

The transformer architecture that underlies essentially all contemporary large language models was designed, and is trained, to model the conditional probability distribution of the next token given its predecessors~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Nothing in this training objective rewards a model for tracking a running numeric total correctly, for refusing to answer when it does not know a fact, or for applying a hard constraint consistently across a long output; each of these is, at best, an emergent side effect of having seen enough examples of correct arithmetic or correct constraint-following during pretraining. Three failure modes that follow from this observation are directly relevant to a dietary recommendation system.

The first is \emph{hallucination}: the tendency of a language model to produce plausible-sounding but false content, including nutrient values for foods it has never been shown data for. Because a hallucinated number looks exactly like a correct one -- both are just a sequence of digit tokens sampled from the model's output distribution -- there is no way to tell them apart from the output alone; the only reliable defence is to never let the model be the source of a number that can instead be looked up or computed. The second is unreliable multi-step arithmetic: even when a model has been shown the individual nutrient values of every ingredient in a recipe, summing them correctly across a full day of several meals, several ingredients each, is a long deterministic computation that transformer-based models perform inconsistently, particularly as the number of terms grows. The third, more subtle, failure mode is \emph{sycophancy}: a tendency to defer to a user's stated preference or explicit request even when doing so conflicts with an earlier, medically motivated constraint. In an evaluation of three widely used commercial models subjected to rebuttals of escalating rhetorical strength across mathematical and medical question answering, \qty{14.66}{\percent} of responses were \emph{regressively} sycophantic -- the model abandoned a correct answer in favour of the user's incorrect one -- and rebuttals citing a fabricated but plausible-sounding source produced the highest regressive rate of any rebuttal type; once a model had conceded, it stayed conceded through the remainder of the rebuttal chain in \qty{78.5}{\percent} of cases~\cite{fanous2025syceval}. For a dietary system that must, for instance, keep a user's sodium intake low regardless of how insistently they ask for something salty, sycophancy is a design hazard as serious as hallucination.

These three failure modes motivate the recurring architectural principle of this thesis: whatever can be checked mechanically should be checked mechanically, and whatever the language model outputs that cannot be checked should be kept as narrowly scoped as possible.

\section{Knowledge-based and neuro-symbolic systems}
\label{sec:background-neuro-symbolic}

A \emph{knowledge-based system} is one whose behaviour is grounded in an explicit, inspectable body of facts and rules rather than solely in statistical patterns learned from training data. Classical dietary knowledge-based systems -- essentially the linear and mixed-integer programmes described in \Cref{sec:background-diet-optimisation} -- are entirely symbolic: their inputs, constraints and outputs are all explicit and auditable, at the cost of being unable to converse, generalise beyond their formalisation, or interpret an ambiguous natural-language request. \emph{Neuro-symbolic} systems combine such symbolic components with a neural one, typically using the neural component for tasks that require flexible pattern recognition or natural-language understanding, and the symbolic component for tasks that require guaranteed correctness or an auditable chain of reasoning.

The architecture in \Cref{cha:design} follows this pattern at the level of individual data items rather than a general theorem prover: the language model produces a structured plan that references food-database entries, and a separate deterministic component sums those entries independently of anything the model claims.

\section{Tool-augmented language model agents}
\label{sec:background-tool-use}

For a language model to consult an external, ground-truth source of information rather than rely on its own parametric memory, it must be able to decide, on its own, when to issue a call to an external function, with what arguments, and how to fold the result back into its ongoing reasoning. Two lines of work established this pattern as standard practice.

Prompting a model to produce an explicit, step-by-step \emph{chain of thought} before its final answer had already been shown to substantially improve its performance on multi-step reasoning problems, without any change to the model's weights~\cite{wei2022chain}, but a chain of thought on its own is still generated entirely from the model's own parametric memory and can only reason about facts and figures the model already believes, correctly or not, to be true. \textsc{ReAct} interleaves such free-text reasoning traces with concrete actions issued to an external environment (for instance, a search-engine query), feeding each action's observation back into the model's context before the next reasoning step~\cite{yao2023react}. This interleaving lets a model revise its plan in light of new information -- for example, retrying a lookup under a different name when the first one returns nothing -- rather than committing to a single, unverified chain of reasoning end to end. On multi-step question-answering benchmarks, \textsc{ReAct} produced markedly fewer hallucinated facts than prompting the model to reason without any tool access at all, precisely because facts it would otherwise have had to invent could instead be retrieved.

\textsc{Toolformer} approaches the same goal from the training side: a model is taught, in a self-supervised manner, to decide for itself which of several available tools (a calculator, a search engine, a calendar) to invoke, with which arguments, and where in its own generated text to splice in the result, without any human-annotated examples of tool use~\cite{schick2023toolformer}. The lesson relevant here is architectural rather than a specific training procedure: a language model does not need to \emph{know} a nutrient value or a running total from its own weights if it can instead be given a well-specified interface to retrieve or compute it.

A closely related mechanism is \emph{structured output}: constraining a model's response to conform to a predeclared schema rather than free text. Structured output does not by itself prevent a wrong or hallucinated value from appearing in a well-typed field, but it does make the output mechanically checkable. How that checkability is used -- an output-validator hook that rejects a food reference whose identifier does not exist -- is the reason the schema-first framework in \Cref{sec:tools-agent-framework} was chosen.

\section{Self-correction: critique-and-refine loops}
\label{sec:background-self-correction}

A second, complementary mechanism for reducing the rate of erroneous outputs is to have a system review and revise its own draft output before returning it, rather than accept whatever is produced from a single generation pass.

\textsc{Self-Refine} formalises this as an iterative loop in which the same model that produced an initial output is prompted, in a second pass, to critique it against the task requirements and then, in a third pass, to revise it in light of that critique, repeating until the critique reports no further issues or an iteration budget is exhausted~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine}. Because the critique and the revision are both produced by the same underlying model with no additional grounding, the approach is easy to implement but offers no guarantee that the critique itself is grounded in anything more reliable than the same statistical patterns that produced the original error.

\textsc{CRITIC} closes this gap by grounding the critique step in external tool feedback rather than in the model's own judgement alone: a draft output is checked against a search engine, a code interpreter, or another verification tool appropriate to the task, and only the resulting, externally verified feedback is used to drive revision~\cite{gou2024critic}. Across question answering, program synthesis and toxicity reduction, tool-grounded critique produced substantially larger gains than critique generated purely by the model reflecting on itself. A closely related study of hallucination in medical question answering formalises the same generate-score-refine pattern with explicit, task-specific thresholds on entailment, factuality and consistency scores that a candidate answer must clear before the loop is allowed to terminate~\cite{ji2023towards}.

Where this thesis sits between those two designs -- a tool-less critic that is still shown the totaller's computed totals -- is a technology choice, taken up in \Cref{sec:tools-self-correction}.

\section{Evaluating generative systems: LLM-as-a-judge and ablation studies}
\label{sec:background-evaluation}

Two methodological building blocks make it possible to evaluate a system like the one in this thesis at all: an automated way to score open-ended, natural-language output, and a controlled way to attribute a change in that score to a specific architectural component.

Reference-based text-generation metrics -- \textsc{BLEU} (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy)~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, devised for machine translation, and \textsc{ROUGE} (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation)~\cite{lin2004rouge}, devised for summarisation -- score a candidate by its n-gram overlap with one or more fixed reference texts, and correlate poorly with human judgement on tasks that reward creativity and diversity rather than surface-level overlap: on the SummEval summarisation benchmark ROUGE-L reaches a summary-level Spearman correlation of only 0.165 with human ratings, and on the Topical-Chat dialogue benchmark ROUGE-L and BLEU-4 reach 0.244 and 0.259~\cite{liu2023geval}. A meal plan's rationale and recipe instructions are exactly this kind of output: there is no single correct phrasing to overlap with, and two equally good plans for the same profile may share almost no vocabulary. \textsc{G-Eval} addresses this by prompting a strong language model to first generate an explicit chain-of-thought evaluation procedure from a natural-language description of the criterion being scored, and then to apply that procedure to the candidate output in a form-filling manner, reporting a probability-weighted score rather than a single sampled integer; on a text-summarisation benchmark this approach achieved a substantially higher correlation with human judgement than prior automatic metrics~\cite{liu2023geval}. A broader study of the same idea, using strong models to judge pairs of chatbot responses against both controlled and crowdsourced human preference data, found that the strongest judge matched human pairwise preferences on \qty{85}{\percent} of non-tied comparisons, against \qty{81}{\percent} agreement between two independent human raters -- where agreement is the probability that two randomly drawn judges of each type would pick the same winner on a randomly drawn question -- while also documenting systematic biases towards longer responses and towards a response's position in a pairwise comparison, and observing that some judges favour their own outputs more often than others without establishing that effect statistically~\cite{zheng2023judging}. The qualitative scoring stage of this thesis follows the G-Eval pattern; the departures -- hand-written steps per criterion, a Monte Carlo estimate of the probability-weighted score, and two judges rather than one -- are specified in \Cref{sec:methodology-qualitative}.

Isolating the contribution of an individual system component calls for the second building block, the \emph{ablation study}: systematically removing or disabling one component of a system at a time while holding everything else constant, and attributing the resulting change in a task-appropriate metric to the removed component. This is the same logic error-attribution studies in machine learning have used for decades, and it is the logic behind the experimental grid in \Cref{sec:methodology-variants}.

\section{Related dietary recommendation systems}
\label{sec:background-related-systems}

Two recent systems combine a language model with an external knowledge source for dietary recommendation in ways directly comparable to the architecture in this thesis. \textsc{NutriGen} builds a personalised nutrition database and relies on prompt engineering, rather than an explicit output schema or a deterministic aggregator, to have the language model incorporate reference values from the USDA nutrition database into a generated meal plan; its own evaluation reports a mean caloric-target error of \qty{1.55}{\percent} for a Llama~3.1~8B backbone against \qty{8.08}{\percent} for the far larger Llama~3.1~70B, and \qty{3.68}{\percent} for GPT-3.5~Turbo against \qty{13.47}{\percent} for GPT-4o, demonstrating that raw model capability is not by itself a reliable predictor of numeric accuracy on this task~\cite{khamesian2025nutrigen}. \textsc{DietQA} takes a structurally different approach, storing recipes, ingredients and inferred dietary tags in a knowledge graph and combining graph-constrained retrieval with a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline so that hard dietary exclusions (an allergen, a diet pattern) are enforced by graph queries before a language model narrates the retrieved, compliant recipes~\cite{tsampos2025dietqa}.

Both systems demonstrate that pairing a language model with an external, queryable knowledge source measurably constrains its output; neither, however, adds a deterministic, independently computed nutrient total that the model itself can consult mid-generation, nor a tool-grounded critique-and-refine loop of the kind described in \Cref{sec:background-self-correction}. The gap this thesis addresses is precisely that combination: a food knowledge base the model must query rather than guess from, a deterministic totaller the model can consult and that a critic can use to ground its review, and an ablation study that isolates how much each of these two mechanisms -- separately and together -- actually contributes to energy and macronutrient accuracy, rather than assuming that grounding a language model in an external source is sufficient on its own.

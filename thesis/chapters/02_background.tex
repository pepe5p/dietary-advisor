\chapter{Background and related work}
\label{cha:background}

The design defended in this thesis is a compromise between two research traditions that have each solved half of the dietary planning problem and neither of which has solved the other half. Operations research settled the arithmetic side of the problem in the 1940s and has been refining it ever since, producing diets that are provably optimal and frequently unappetising. Natural language processing has, much more recently, produced systems that understand what a person is asking for and can write a recipe they might enjoy, but that cannot be trusted to add up the recipe's contents. This chapter reviews both traditions and the machinery that has grown up between them: the failure modes that make an unaided language model unsuitable for this task, the tool-use and self-correction techniques that partially mitigate those failures, the food composition data any grounded system must build on, and the evaluation methods that make claims about such systems measurable. It closes with the recent dietary systems this work is most directly comparable to, and with the specific gap it sets out to fill.

\section{Diet planning as a mathematical optimisation problem}
\label{sec:background-diet-optimisation}

Long before language models existed, dietary planning was already recognised as a problem with a precise mathematical structure. In 1945, the economist George Stigler posed the question of the cheapest combination of 77 foods that would satisfy the recommended daily allowances of nine nutrients for an active man, and solved it by heuristic elimination to within a few cents of the true optimum~\cite{stigler1945cost}. Two years after linear programming and the simplex algorithm were formalised, George Dantzig used Stigler's own data as one of the first real-world test cases for the new method, confirming the heuristic solution and turning the \emph{diet problem} into a standard illustration of linear programming to this day~\cite{dantzig1990diet}. The formulation is simple: nutrient requirements become linear inequality constraints, food quantities become continuous decision variables, and a cost or deviation term becomes the objective to minimise.

Subsequent work refined the formulation rather than the underlying idea. Sklan and Dariel extended the linear programme to mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) so that foods eaten in whole units, and higher-level constraints such as meal structure or exchange-group frequency, could be represented directly~\cite{sklan1993diet}. A 2018 review of fifty-two studies applying linear programming to diet problems found that the overwhelming majority impose only nutritional and cost constraints, with far fewer incorporating environmental or -- crucially -- palatability and acceptability constraints, and concluded that no study had yet offered a satisfactory way to encode how acceptable a mathematically optimal diet actually is to the person who has to eat it~\cite{vandooren2018review}. This is the central limitation of the classical approach: an LP or MILP solver finds the numerically optimal diet with respect to whatever is written into its constraint matrix, but it has no notion of a recipe, a cuisine, or a craving, and encoding those notions as linear constraints is, at best, an approximation of something inherently linguistic and personal.

This gap is precisely where large language models are attractive: they excel at exactly the semantic, preference-laden reasoning that a solver cannot express, while a solver (or, as in this thesis, a lighter-weight deterministic aggregator over a curated food database) excels at exactly the arithmetic a language model cannot be trusted to perform unaided. A 2026 study combining GPT-4o with a MILP solver over a shared food catalogue makes this trade-off explicit: an LLM alone was rated by domain experts as the least accurate of three approaches on a nutrient-accuracy scale, a MILP solver alone was the most numerically accurate but the least personalised and among the least practical, and a pipeline in which the LLM filters candidate foods for the solver and then narrates the solver's output scored close to the MILP-only baseline on accuracy while matching or exceeding the LLM-only baseline on personalisation and practicality~\cite{alkeyeva2026chatgpt}. The system studied in this thesis follows the same division of labour without introducing a general-purpose solver: the language model proposes recipes and quantities from a curated catalogue, and a deterministic aggregator -- described in \Cref{sec:design-totaller} -- independently recomputes every nutrient total the model claims.

\section{Failure modes of large language models in numeric and safety-critical tasks}
\label{sec:background-llm-failure-modes}

The transformer architecture that underlies essentially all contemporary large language models was designed, and is trained, to model the conditional probability distribution of the next token given its predecessors~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Nothing in this training objective rewards a model for tracking a running numeric total correctly, for refusing to answer when it does not know a fact, or for applying a hard constraint consistently across a long output; each of these is, at best, an emergent side effect of having seen enough examples of correct arithmetic or correct constraint-following during pretraining. Three failure modes that follow from this observation are directly relevant to a dietary recommendation system.

The first is \emph{hallucination}: the tendency of a language model to produce plausible-sounding but false content, including nutrient values for foods it has never been shown data for. Because a hallucinated number looks exactly like a correct one -- both are just a sequence of digit tokens sampled from the model's output distribution -- there is no way to tell them apart from the output alone; the only reliable defence is to never let the model be the source of a number that can instead be looked up or computed. The second is unreliable multi-step arithmetic: even when a model has been shown the individual nutrient values of every ingredient in a recipe, summing them correctly across a full day of several meals, several ingredients each, is a long deterministic computation that transformer-based models perform inconsistently, particularly as the number of terms grows. The third, more subtle, failure mode is \emph{sycophancy}: a tendency to defer to a user's stated preference or explicit request even when doing so conflicts with an earlier, medically motivated constraint. In an evaluation of large language models responding to leading or incorrect user assertions in medical question answering, some widely used commercial models conceded to an incorrect user claim more than \qty{62}{\percent} of the time, and reinforced the incorrect claim more strongly once the user cited a plausible-sounding source for it~\cite{ji2023towards}. For a dietary system that must, for instance, keep a user's sodium intake low regardless of how insistently they ask for something salty, sycophancy is a design hazard as serious as hallucination.

These three failure modes motivate the recurring architectural principle of this thesis: whatever can be checked mechanically should be checked mechanically, and whatever the language model outputs that cannot be checked should be kept as narrowly scoped as possible.

\section{Knowledge-based and neuro-symbolic systems}
\label{sec:background-neuro-symbolic}

A \emph{knowledge-based system} is one whose behaviour is grounded in an explicit, inspectable body of facts and rules rather than solely in statistical patterns learned from training data. Classical dietary knowledge-based systems -- essentially the linear and mixed-integer programmes described in \Cref{sec:background-diet-optimisation} -- are entirely symbolic: their inputs, constraints and outputs are all explicit and auditable, at the cost of being unable to converse, generalise beyond their formalisation, or interpret an ambiguous natural-language request. \emph{Neuro-symbolic} systems combine such symbolic components with a neural one, typically using the neural component for tasks that require flexible pattern recognition or natural-language understanding, and the symbolic component for tasks that require guaranteed correctness or an auditable chain of reasoning.

The system developed in this thesis follows this pattern at the level of individual data items rather than a general theorem prover: the language model interprets the user's profile and free-form request and produces a structured meal plan that references, rather than invents, entries in a food composition knowledge base (\Cref{sec:background-food-data}); a separate deterministic component sums the referenced entries' nutrient values and reports the result independently of anything the model claims. This keeps the symbolic guarantee narrow -- every number in the final report is traceable to a database row and an arithmetic operation, not to a language model's internal weights -- while leaving the much harder problem of natural-language understanding, recipe composition and preference reasoning to the component best suited for it.

\section{Tool-augmented language model agents}
\label{sec:background-tool-use}

For a language model to consult an external, ground-truth source of information rather than rely on its own parametric memory, it must be able to decide, on its own, when to issue a call to an external function, with what arguments, and how to fold the result back into its ongoing reasoning. Two lines of work established this pattern as standard practice.

Prompting a model to produce an explicit, step-by-step \emph{chain of thought} before its final answer had already been shown to substantially improve its performance on multi-step reasoning problems, without any change to the model's weights~\cite{wei2022chain}, but a chain of thought on its own is still generated entirely from the model's own parametric memory and can only reason about facts and figures the model already believes, correctly or not, to be true. \textsc{ReAct} interleaves such free-text reasoning traces with concrete actions issued to an external environment (for instance, a search-engine query), feeding each action's observation back into the model's context before the next reasoning step~\cite{yao2023react}. This interleaving lets a model revise its plan in light of new information -- for example, retrying a lookup under a different name when the first one returns nothing -- rather than committing to a single, unverified chain of reasoning end to end. On multi-step question-answering benchmarks, \textsc{ReAct} produced markedly fewer hallucinated facts than prompting the model to reason without any tool access at all, precisely because facts it would otherwise have had to invent could instead be retrieved.

\textsc{Toolformer} approaches the same goal from the training side: a model is taught, in a self-supervised manner, to decide for itself which of several available tools (a calculator, a search engine, a calendar) to invoke, with which arguments, and where in its own generated text to splice in the result, without any human-annotated examples of tool use~\cite{schick2023toolformer}. The lesson relevant to this thesis is architectural rather than a specific training procedure: a language model does not need to \emph{know} a nutrient value or a running total from its own weights if it can instead be given a well-specified interface to retrieve or compute it, and can be trained or prompted to prefer doing so. The nutrition agent in this thesis (\Cref{sec:design-agents}) is built on exactly this premise, exposed through the structured tool-calling interface of a modern agent framework rather than through the token-level splicing scheme of the original Toolformer work.

A closely related and, for a system whose contract explicitly forbids inventing food data, equally important mechanism is \emph{structured output}: constraining a model's response to conform to a predeclared schema (in this system, a Pydantic model validated on every generation) rather than free text. Structured output does not by itself prevent a wrong or hallucinated value from appearing in a well-typed field, but it does make the output mechanically checkable -- a validator can, for instance, reject a food reference whose identifier does not exist in the database before the response is ever shown to a user, which is exactly how the no-invented-foods contract in \Cref{sec:design-no-invented-foods} is enforced.

\section{Self-correction: critique-and-refine loops}
\label{sec:background-self-correction}

A second, complementary mechanism for reducing the rate of erroneous outputs is to have a system review and revise its own draft output before returning it, rather than accept whatever is produced from a single generation pass.

\textsc{Self-Refine} formalises this as an iterative loop in which the same model that produced an initial output is prompted, in a second pass, to critique it against the task requirements and then, in a third pass, to revise it in light of that critique, repeating until the critique reports no further issues or an iteration budget is exhausted~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine}. Because the critique and the revision are both produced by the same underlying model with no additional grounding, the approach is easy to implement but offers no guarantee that the critique itself is grounded in anything more reliable than the same statistical patterns that produced the original error.

\textsc{CRITIC} closes this gap by grounding the critique step in external tool feedback rather than in the model's own judgement alone: a draft output is checked against a search engine, a code interpreter, or another verification tool appropriate to the task, and only the resulting, externally verified feedback is used to drive revision~\cite{gou2024critic}. Across question answering, program synthesis and toxicity reduction, tool-grounded critique produced substantially larger gains than critique generated purely by the model reflecting on itself. A closely related study of hallucination in medical question answering formalises the same generate-score-refine pattern with explicit, task-specific thresholds on entailment, factuality and consistency scores that a candidate answer must clear before the loop is allowed to terminate~\cite{ji2023towards}.

The reflection loop in this thesis (\Cref{sec:design-reflection}) sits between these two designs. Its critic is not tool-augmented in the sense of \textsc{CRITIC} -- it does not issue further food lookups -- but it is not unaided either: when the deterministic totaller is enabled, the critic is shown its independently computed nutrient totals and coverage warnings alongside the proposed plan, so that its judgement of whether a macronutrient target has been met is grounded in a computed number rather than in the same model's own arithmetic. \Cref{cha:results} reports how much this grounding, and the critique-and-refine loop as a whole, contribute to plan accuracy relative to a single-pass generation.

\section{Food composition data sources}
\label{sec:background-food-data}

Any grounding strategy of the kind described above is only as good as the knowledge base it grounds against. Two large, openly available food composition sources cover complementary needs. The USDA FoodData Central Foundation Foods and SR Legacy datasets provide laboratory-analysed nutrient profiles for generic, minimally processed foods (a raw carrot, a whole egg, rolled oats) with broad international applicability but no coverage of specific branded or packaged products. Open Food Facts is a crowd-sourced, international database of branded, packaged products, contributed by volunteers who photograph and transcribe product labels; it has far denser coverage of the kind of packaged food a consumer actually buys, but far less consistent completeness of any individual nutrient field, since it depends on what a given contributor chose to transcribe. \Cref{sec:design-food-db} describes how the two sources are combined behind a single lookup interface, and \Cref{sec:impl-missing-nutrients} discusses in detail how the resulting patchy coverage is handled without silently treating a missing value as zero.

\section{Evaluating generative systems: LLM-as-a-judge and ablation studies}
\label{sec:background-evaluation}

Two methodological building blocks make it possible to evaluate a system like the one in this thesis at all: an automated way to score open-ended, natural-language output, and a controlled way to attribute a change in that score to a specific architectural component.

Reference-based text-generation metrics such as BLEU or ROUGE correlate poorly with human judgement on tasks that reward creativity or personalisation rather than surface-level overlap with a fixed reference, which is precisely the kind of task a meal plan's rationale and recipe instructions represent. \textsc{G-Eval} addresses this by prompting a strong language model to first generate an explicit chain-of-thought evaluation procedure from a natural-language description of the criterion being scored, and then to apply that procedure to the candidate output in a form-filling manner, reporting a probability-weighted score rather than a single sampled integer; on a text-summarisation benchmark this approach achieved a substantially higher correlation with human judgement than prior automatic metrics~\cite{liu2023geval}. A broader study of the same idea, using strong models to judge pairs of chatbot responses against both controlled and crowdsourced human preference data, found agreement between the strongest judge models and human raters exceeding \qty{80}{\percent}, comparable to the level of agreement between two independent human raters, while also documenting systematic judge biases -- towards longer responses, towards a response's position in a pairwise comparison, and towards a judge's own model family -- that any LLM-as-a-judge protocol must account for~\cite{zheng2023judging}. The qualitative scoring stage of the evaluation harness in this thesis (\Cref{sec:methodology-qualitative}) follows the G-Eval pattern, with an explicit rubric per soft criterion and a judge model kept fixed and distinct from the models under evaluation.

Isolating the contribution of an individual system component calls for the second building block, the \emph{ablation study}: systematically removing or disabling one component of a system at a time (or, in this thesis, one at a time from a fixed baseline) while holding everything else constant, and attributing the resulting change in a task-appropriate metric to the removed component. This is the same logic error-attribution studies in machine learning have used for decades, and it is the logic behind the four-variant experimental grid in \Cref{cha:methodology}: baseline, totaller-only, reflection-only, and both mechanisms enabled together, evaluated on the same fixed set of scenarios so that any difference in macronutrient error between variants is attributable only to the mechanisms that were toggled.

\section{Related dietary recommendation systems}
\label{sec:background-related-systems}

Two recent systems combine a language model with an external knowledge source for dietary recommendation in ways directly comparable to the architecture in this thesis. \textsc{NutriGen} builds a personalised nutrition database and relies on prompt engineering, rather than an explicit output schema or a deterministic aggregator, to have the language model incorporate reference values from the USDA nutrition database into a generated meal plan; its own evaluation reports the lowest caloric-target percentage error, \qty{1.55}{\percent}, for a Llama~3.1~8B backbone, but a substantially higher error, \qty{10.45}{\percent} mean absolute error, for a larger, more capable model, demonstrating that raw model capability is not by itself a reliable predictor of numeric accuracy on this task~\cite{khamesian2025nutrigen}. \textsc{DietQA} takes a structurally different approach, storing recipes, ingredients and inferred dietary tags in a knowledge graph and combining graph-constrained retrieval with a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline so that hard dietary exclusions (an allergen, a diet pattern) are enforced by graph queries before a language model narrates the retrieved, compliant recipes~\cite{tsampos2025dietqa}.

Both systems demonstrate that pairing a language model with an external, queryable knowledge source measurably constrains its output; neither, however, adds a deterministic, independently computed nutrient total that the model itself can consult mid-generation, nor a tool-grounded critique-and-refine loop of the kind described in \Cref{sec:background-self-correction}. The gap this thesis addresses is precisely that combination: a food knowledge base the model must query rather than guess from, a deterministic totaller the model can consult and that a critic can use to ground its review, and an ablation study that isolates how much each of these two mechanisms -- separately and together -- actually contributes to macronutrient accuracy, rather than assuming that grounding a language model in an external source is sufficient on its own.

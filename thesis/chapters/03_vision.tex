\chapter{Vision of the solution}
\label{cha:vision}

This chapter sets out what the system is meant to do before any implementation decision is made. Everything stated here is a requirement the design in \Cref{cha:design} must satisfy, not a description of what was eventually built. Two of the requirements stated in this chapter are worth flagging in advance, because they shape the architecture far more than the rest. The first is that the system may never report a nutrient figure it cannot trace to a database entry, which turns out to be enforceable structurally rather than by instruction and drives the whole output schema. The second is that the grounding and review mechanisms under study must be independently switchable without disturbing anything else, since the entire empirical contribution of this work depends on being able to attribute a measured difference to one toggled mechanism. The chapter also settles, and justifies, one question that shapes the experiments: which parts of the system are legitimate experimental variables at all.

\section{Usage scenario}
\label{sec:vision-usage-scenario}

The intended interaction is short and self-contained. A user's profile -- age, sex, body measurements, activity level, a dietary goal, any allergies or diagnosed conditions, and any standing food preferences or dislikes -- will already be on file, since none of it changes from one session to the next. At the start of a session the system will ask the user to describe, in free text, anything specific they want for that day (an ingredient to use up, a craving, a time constraint) or to proceed with no additional request at all. From this input alone, the system will produce one complete plan for one day: a fixed number of meals, each with a name, a step-by-step preparation method, and the exact grams of every ingredient it uses. That day of meals is the deliverable. The system will not carry on a multi-turn conversation that incrementally edits a previously generated plan, nor will it remember what a user actually ate afterwards; each session produces one plan from a stated set of inputs. Neither omission reflects a difficulty: both are essentially a matter of carrying state between sessions, and neither introduces a mechanism whose contribution this thesis sets out to measure, so both were deliberately left out rather than attempted and abandoned (\Cref{sec:impl-dead-ends}).

\section{Requirements}
\label{sec:vision-requirements}

The requirements below are what the design of \Cref{cha:design} must satisfy. They are grouped by the kind of claim each one makes: what a plan must always be, what it must try to be, and what the system must permit in order to be studied at all.

\begin{description}
    \freq{req:one-day-plan} The system shall accept a fixed user profile (demographic data, dietary goal, allergies, medical conditions, food preferences) and an optional free-form session request, and shall produce a single, complete plan covering every meal of one day.
    \freq{req:recipes-and-grams} Every meal in the plan shall include a named recipe with preparation instructions detailed enough for a home cook to follow, and a list of ingredients with their exact quantities in grams.
    \freq{req:no-invented-nutrients} Every ingredient quantity in the plan shall correspond to a real entry in a food composition database rather than to a value invented by the system; the system shall never report a nutrient figure that cannot be traced to a database entry and a specific quantity.
\end{description}

The system can be built so that a plan violating any of these three never reaches a user: it is rejected before delivery if a meal is missing, a recipe field is empty, or any food it names fails to resolve in the database. \ref{req:no-invented-nutrients}~in particular holds absolutely rather than on average.

The next two are goals rather than guarantees, because both rest on the model's judgement about food. An exclusion has to be read off a food's name, category and ingredient text, the food data carrying no allergen or diet-pattern tag reliable enough to filter on, and a target can only be aimed at. Both are therefore measured per plan -- by the safety score of \Cref{def:safety-score} and the accuracy results of \Cref{cha:results} -- rather than enforced.

\begin{description}
    \freq{req:constraints} No meal in the plan shall include a food that conflicts with a hard exclusion stated in the user's profile (an allergen, or a diet pattern such as vegetarianism), and the plan shall take reasonable account of soft preferences (liked and disliked foods, a session request) where doing so does not conflict with a hard exclusion or a medical constraint.
    \freq{req:macro-targets} The plan shall target the energy and macronutrient levels implied by the user's profile and dietary goal; the targets themselves, and the delivered totals they are compared against, shall be computed independently of the language model's own claims about either.
\end{description}

The last three constrain the system rather than the plan. Two make the ablation study possible -- \ref{req:ablatable}~is the one the entire empirical contribution of this work rests on -- and the third caps how long a session may take.

\begin{description}
    \nfreq{req:ablatable} The system's behaviour shall be reproducible and measurable well enough to support a controlled ablation study: individual grounding and review mechanisms shall be independently switchable without altering anything else about the pipeline, so that a difference in outcome between two configurations can be attributed to the mechanism that was toggled.
    \nfreq{req:provider-agnostic} The system shall be usable with any of several LLM providers, and shall expose the reasoning-effort setting a provider offers as a configurable parameter, so that the effect of model capability on plan quality can be measured along both axes without being tied to a single vendor's model family.
    \nfreq{req:interactive-runtime} Every user-visible run of the system shall complete within a time compatible with an interactive session, since a plan that takes many minutes to produce would not be a viable substitute for existing planning workflows.
\end{description}

\section{Food lookup as a fixed baseline, not an experimental variable}
\label{sec:vision-food-lookup-baseline}

An early design question was whether the ablation study should also test a variant of the system with no food database access at all -- that is, a language model reasoning about nutrient values purely from its own training data. This variant was deliberately excluded from the experimental grid, and the decisive reason is that the two arms could not be placed on one scale. The quantitative metric this thesis reports (\Cref{def:macro-error}) is computed by a deterministic aggregator from portions that have been resolved against the real database, so a variant with no database access supplies nothing the metric can be computed from. Its plan names foods without identifying them, and a food that is not identified has no known composition: there is simply nothing to aggregate, and its error against a target is not a quantity this evaluation can determine at all. Nor can the gap be closed after the fact. Matching those names to database entries once the plan is finished would mean the resulting figure would describe how well the post-hoc matcher guessed what the model meant rather than how good the plan was. That leaves only the model's own statement of its totals, which is not a measurement of anything and cannot be placed on the same axis as one.

Two further reasons point the same way. \ref{req:no-invented-nutrients}~makes food lookup a correctness requirement, so a configuration without it is not a weaker version of the same system but one that violates its own contract. And the outcome is not in question: that a system reading real nutrient data reports nutrient values more accurately than one recalling them is a foregone conclusion rather than an open research question. The ablation study in \Cref{cha:methodology} therefore treats the food knowledge base as always present, and varies only the deterministic totaller and the critique-and-refine loop, both of which are optional enhancements on top of a system that already respects \ref{req:no-invented-nutrients}~in every configuration.

\section{Success criteria}
\label{sec:vision-success-criteria}

The system succeeds on two counts: that it satisfies the requirements of \Cref{sec:vision-requirements} -- those that can be enforced holding on every run, the rest measured and reported rather than assumed -- and that it can answer the questions asked of it, with the deterministic totaller and the critique-and-refine loop switchable in isolation so that every hypothesis of \Cref{sec:introduction-goal} gets a verdict. The second is a condition on the instrument rather than on the findings: a mechanism found to contribute nothing satisfies it exactly as well as one found to help.

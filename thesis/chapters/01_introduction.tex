\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cha:introduction}

Planning what one person will eat over a single day combines two tasks with very different demands. One is linguistic and open-ended: interpreting a loosely worded request, respecting a set of personal and medical constraints, and arriving at food the person will realistically prepare and eat. The other is arithmetic and closed: keeping running totals of calories and nutrients inside prescribed bounds. A large language model is well suited to the first and unreliable at the second: the computation is incidental to what the model was trained to do, and for a person managing a chronic condition a nutrient total that is confidently wrong is a safety problem, not an inconvenience. This thesis takes that division as its starting point and asks a measurable question: if a language model is required to plan meals only from a real food knowledge base, how much additional reliability follows from giving it a deterministic nutrient totaller, from adding an automated review pass, and from doing both at once, and how do those answers change with the choice of model and reasoning-effort level (\Cref{sec:tools-model-access})?

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:introduction-motivation}

Diet is among the modifiable factors public health guidance most consistently ties to preventing chronic disease, and among the hardest for individuals to act on. The gap is rarely a lack of information: national dietary guidelines, calorie tables and nutrient databases are freely available, yet turning them into meals a specific person will realistically cook and eat -- with a specific set of allergies, medical conditions and food preferences -- is a planning problem most people are neither trained nor willing to solve by hand. Registered dietitians solve it professionally, but their time is expensive and their availability limited, which motivates the long-standing interest in automated dietary recommendation systems.

Large language models (LLMs) are an attractive engine for this kind of personalisation: a modern instruction-tuned model can hold a natural conversation about food preferences, reason about the interaction between a medical condition and a nutrient, and explain its recommendations, without the rigid input forms older expert systems required. This fluency is a consequence of the architecture that produces it: the transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} underlying a modern language model is trained to predict the next token given its predecessors~\cite{brown2020language}, not to perform verified arithmetic or enforce a hard constraint. Asked to plan a full day of meals, it must simultaneously invent recipes and keep a running total of calories, protein, sodium and other nutrients against numeric targets -- a role it was never trained to play reliably. Recent evaluations confirm this empirically: even capable general-purpose models can miss calorie targets by a two-digit percentage, and larger open-weight models are not automatically better at the arithmetic than smaller ones~\cite{khamesian2025nutrigen}.

The severity of such errors depends on who the plan is for. A calorie overshoot for a healthy adult is a nuisance; the same error for a user managing type~2 diabetes and hypertension, where sodium and glycaemic load must stay within tight, medically motivated bounds, is a safety issue. A system making credible use of an LLM in this domain therefore cannot treat the model as the sole source of truth for anything checkable: it must keep the model's fluency where it is useful (interpreting a request, proposing a recipe, explaining a rationale) and replace it with deterministic computation and verified data wherever a wrong answer is consequential. This motivates combining an LLM with a \emph{knowledge-based} system: a curated food knowledge base the model must query instead of guessing from, and a deterministic aggregation routine (the \emph{totaller} of \Cref{sec:design-totaller}) that computes nutrient totals independently of anything the model claims.

\section{Goal and hypotheses}
\label{sec:introduction-goal}

This thesis designs, implements and empirically evaluates such a hybrid system: an LLM-based agent for one-day meal planning grounded in a real food knowledge base, checked by a deterministic nutrient totaller and reviewed by an automated critique-and-refine loop. It measures how much each of the latter two mechanisms contributes to the accuracy and reliability of the resulting plans.

The food knowledge base is a premise of the investigation, not one of its variables (\Cref{sec:vision-food-lookup-premise}); the deterministic totaller and the review loop are the optional, testable mechanisms. The investigation is organised around three hypotheses.

\begin{description}
    \item[H1] Letting the planning agent consult a deterministic nutrient totaller while composing a plan reduces the error between the requested and the delivered energy and macronutrient targets, relative to the same food-grounded system with the totaller disabled.
    \item[H2] Adding an automated critique-and-refine loop -- in which a reviewing pass checks the plan against the user's medical constraints and preferences and a refining pass revises it where the review finds fault -- improves adherence to the user's medical constraints and food preferences, relative to the same system with the loop disabled.
    \item[H3] Under a fixed system configuration, the quality of the delivered plans depends on both the choice of language model and the reasoning-effort level that model is configured to use (\Cref{sec:tools-model-access}), and the two are partly interchangeable: raising the reasoning-effort level of a smaller model recovers a substantial part of the gap to a larger one.
\end{description}

H1~and H2~are tested by an ablation study (\Cref{cha:methodology,cha:results}) that switches the totaller and the critique-and-refine loop independently, giving four configurations, across a fixed set of clinically motivated test scenarios. H3~is tested by a second experiment that holds the system at its fullest configuration and varies the language model and its reasoning-effort level together.

\section{Scope and non-goals}
\label{sec:introduction-scope}

The system produces a single, complete one-day meal plan from a fixed user profile and an optional free-form request; it is not a multi-turn assistant that incrementally edits a plan, and it does not track meals actually eaten over time. The nutrient targets cover energy, macronutrients and a fixed set of micronutrients (\Cref{sec:design-food-db}); the system is not a substitute for an individualised consultation with a dietitian or physician, and the medical scenarios used for evaluation are a deliberately narrow slice of clinical complexity, chosen for their well-documented dietary guidance instead of for exhaustive coverage. A graphical user interface, multi-language support in the generated plans, and long-term adherence tracking are left outside scope, as none bear on the central research question.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction-contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A neuro-symbolic architecture (\Cref{sec:background-neuro-symbolic}) for LLM-based meal planning in which the language model has no field to write a nutrient value into; every nutrient figure the system reports as data is computed by the deterministic totaller (\Cref{sec:design-totaller}) from a retrieved, traceable food knowledge base entry, so numeric hallucination is closed off by the output schema and not by an instruction in the prompt.
    \item An ablation study isolating the individual and combined contribution of the deterministic totaller and a critique-and-refine loop to energy and macronutrient accuracy and to preference and safety adherence, across scenarios that differ in how tightly they constrain the plan, and extended into a comparison across several language models and reasoning-effort settings.
    \item An evaluation protocol that treats its own measuring instrument as suspect: every plan is scored by two independent judge models from different vendors, each scoring every plan several times, so that judge noise and vendor-specific bias are visible in the results instead of being folded into them.
    \item A documented account of the engineering difficulties of grounding a language model in a heterogeneous, incomplete real-world food knowledge base -- partial nutrient coverage, hybrid lexical-semantic retrieval, and tool-call ergonomics under a limited context budget -- and the design decisions used to address them.
\end{itemize}

The source code of the system and of the evaluation harness is published online.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/pepe5p/dietary-advisor}}

\section{Thesis outline}
\label{sec:introduction-outline}

\Cref{cha:background} reviews the mathematical origins of diet optimisation, the failure modes of language models that motivate a hybrid design, and the tool-use and self-correction techniques the system builds on. \Cref{cha:vision} states the vision and requirements for the system before any implementation choice is made. \Cref{cha:methods-and-tools} surveys the candidate technologies for each layer of the system and justifies the choices carried into the design. \Cref{cha:design} presents the resulting architecture. \Cref{cha:implementation} walks through the concrete difficulties met while building the system and the reasoning behind each solution. \Cref{cha:methodology} defines the experimental protocol -- including the evaluation harness -- and the metrics used to test the hypotheses, and \Cref{cha:results} reports the results and discusses threats to validity. \Cref{cha:conclusions} closes the thesis with a summary of findings and directions for further work. \Cref{cha:worked-example} walks through one complete session.
